# Module 2: Communication, Presence and Influence

> [!abstract] Module in one line
> How do I build relationships that lead to great results? Module 2 builds the intra and interpersonal skills to become a flexible communicator: observant of difference, aware of emotions (your own and others'), and able to consciously respond rather than react.

## Learning Intentions

- Recognise your own people style.
- Develop strategies to improve communication.
- Have a conversation using the Ladder of Inference.
- Match mood to task in meetings.
- Develop the capacity to build an action plan to improve emotional intelligence skills.

> [!note] Three themes
> The module covers why communication matters (its direct impact on outcomes), the **People Styles** model (understanding your own and others' styles), and the **Ladder of Inference** (understanding your own thinking, reactions and how you make sense of the world). The objective is to become a flexible communicator who is observant, accepting of difference, and able to manage emotions.

## 2.1 The Johari Window

Communication underpins all relationships, and performance often hinges on communication alignment with the people you work with (internal and external stakeholders). The **Johari Window**, created by **Joseph Luft and Harry Ingham (1955)**, examines your own role in communicating with others. The name is a combination of their first names (Joe + Harry).

Two dynamics create four quadrants: what is known / unknown to the self, and what is known / made known to others. Because the model is about what *you* communicate, the "others" dimension is about what you make known to others, not what they already know.

```
                    Known to self        Unknown to self
                 +-------------------+-------------------+
   Known to      |   PUBLIC ARENA    |    BLIND SPOT     |
   others        | (open, shared)    | (others see it,   |
                 | all the action    |  you don't)       |
                 +-------------------+-------------------+
   Unknown to    |   FACADE / MASK   |     UNKNOWN       |
   others        | (you keep it in)  | (creative space,  |
                 |                   |  untapped)        |
                 +-------------------+-------------------+
```

| Quadrant | Definition | Notes from the material |
|---|---|---|
| **Public Arena** | Known to self and others | Where all the action takes place. The larger the arena, the better the communication and the better the results. |
| **Blind Spot** | Known to others, unknown to self | What others can see but you can't; what others think of you that you aren't aware of. |
| **Facade (Mask)** | Known to self, unknown to others | What you keep from others (useful in negotiation, but often driven by fear of embarrassment or hidden motives). Maintaining it takes energy. |
| **Unknown** | Unknown to self and others | A creative space uncovered in powerful conversations; untapped potential. |

**Two levers to grow the public arena:**

- **Inquiry** (ask for feedback, ask questions) grows the arena to the right and reduces your **blind spot**.
- **Advocacy** (disclose more, share your point of view) grows the arena downwards and reduces your **facade**.

**Four archetypes** (labels are for understanding the concept only, not for describing others):

| Archetype | Behaviour | Consequence |
|---|---|---|
| **Small public arena (Oyster)** | Asks few questions, discloses little | Useful for focus, but limits high quality communication and results. |
| **Large facade (Interview)** | Asks many questions, discloses little | Can breed distrust: "what's the agenda?" Useful for a journalist or auditor, but not engaging. |
| **Lecturer** | Discloses readily, asks few questions | Useful for teaching, but one way; the other person disengages and the blind spot stays large. |
| **Full engagement** | Invests in both inquiry and advocacy | The aim: maximises sharing, understanding, clarity and results. |

> [!tip] Key insight
> There is a direct correlation between the size of the public arena and the results you receive. The way to grow it is more inquiry (ask for feedback) and more advocacy (disclose more). Awareness is the starting point.

> [!caution] Cautions when using it (from the additional reading)
> The Johari Window has a limited evidence base and measurement is subjective. The "unknown" quadrant can surface repressed or distressing material, so aftercare (coaching or therapy) is recommended and it should not replace clinical care. Its usefulness also depends on a culture of psychological safety: without it, people won't feel able to be authentic. Only 10 to 15% of people actually meet the criteria for being self-aware.

## 2.2 Exploring People Styles

Psychologists **David Merrill and Roger Reid (1999)**, with the model further developed by **Robert Bolton and Dorothy Grover Bolton**, offer the **Four People Styles** model to improve workplace communication, collaboration and leadership. People differ in how they think, decide, use time, handle emotions, manage stress and deal with conflict. Understanding this reduces conflict, creates influence and builds high functioning teams.

> [!important] Three ground rules
> No style is better or worse, they are simply different. We are all four styles, but most of us have a dominant style. Your style does not excuse or exclude behaviour; it is a useful behavioural marker, not a box.

The model sits on two axes:

- **Assertiveness** (horizontal): the degree to which behaviour is seen as direct or forceful. *Less assertive* = gestures less, slower, leans back, speaks softly, slower to decide. *More assertive* = vigorous gestures, more eye contact, moves and speaks rapidly, decides quickly, more confrontational.
- **Responsiveness** (vertical): the degree to which behaviour shows emotion and awareness of others' feelings. *Less responsive* = reserved, task oriented, uses facts and logic, formal, structured with time. *More responsive* = expressive, friendly, uses stories, people oriented, casual, less structured with time.

```
                       Less Responsive
                            |
        ANALYTICAL          |          DRIVING
   (less assertive,         |     (more assertive,
    less responsive)        |      less responsive)
   Less Assertive ----------+---------- More Assertive
        AMIABLE             |          EXPRESSIVE
   (less assertive,         |     (more assertive,
    more responsive)        |      more responsive)
                            |
                       More Responsive
```

| Style | Axes | Characteristics | Strength / call on them for |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Driving** | More assertive, less responsive | Fast, efficient, outcome focused, blunt, tell oriented, facts over feelings, "wanted it yesterday" | Getting things done |
| **Expressive** | More assertive, more responsive | Flamboyant, visionary, energetic, loves the limelight, impulsive, weak on detail and follow through | Innovation and inspiration |
| **Amiable** | Less assertive, more responsive | People oriented, friendly, empathetic, easygoing, reluctant to offend, slower to form opinions | Reading where the team is at (the barometer) |
| **Analytical** | Less assertive, less responsive | Values exactness and quality, systematic, perfectionist, by the book, agonises over decisions | Keeping you out of harm's way |

> [!tip] Key insight
> No single style correlates with leadership. Awareness enables leadership. Effective leaders surround themselves with other styles to get diversity of thought. Learn about the style you find most challenging to work with. Beware stereotype bias: all genders appear in all four quadrants.

## 2.3 The Ladder of Inference

The **Ladder of Inference**, developed by **Chris Argyris (1985)**, explains how we move very quickly from raw data to action, often on little data and many assumptions. The version taught here is a slightly more accessible version that makes it easier to discuss emotions.

We notice some data and filter out the rest, then tell ourselves a **story** to make sense of it. That story drives what we **think and feel**, which drives what we **say and do**. Sometimes the conclusions are accurate, often they are not. The story can build its own momentum and start to sound like the truth.

```
   ^  SAY AND DO        Actions
   |  THINK AND FEEL    Conclusions, judgments, emotions
   |  STORY             Assumptions, beliefs, experiences, meaning
   |  SEE AND HEAR      Data (the safest place is at the bottom)
```

**The worked example:** Suzi says good morning, Sally doesn't respond. Within a second the story unfolds ("she was aloof yesterday, I've gone out of my way for her...") leading to a conclusion ("I'll pull back my support") and an action. Staying on the ground longer would have surfaced more data: Sally had headphones in and simply didn't hear.

**To change your actions, come down the ladder:**

1. Check your assumptions.
2. Suspend your beliefs.
3. Check the data that you selected.
4. Re-examine your interpretation of the data.

> [!quote] Viktor E. Frankl
> Between stimulus and response there is a space. In that space is our power to choose our response. In our response lies our growth and our freedom.

> [!tip] Key insight
> The most powerful way to improve communication is to take control of your story. Stay on the ground and gather more data: it is the safest place to be. Be prepared to challenge your own story and check that what you think are facts really are facts.

> [!note] Related activity
> The **Organisational Detective Activity** (walking into a new Director's office and forming impressions from the objects there) demonstrates the ladder in action: what conclusions do you form, what data are they based on, and what alternative interpretations exist?

## 2.4 The Ladder of Inference: Relationships and Conversations

**Applied to relationships:** imagine two ladders, yours and theirs. Their top rung (what they say and do) influences what you see and hear, which feeds your story, your feelings and your actions, which then feeds their ladder. The cycle can become vicious. You can change the dynamic by coming down *your own* ladder: gather more data, suspend your story and judgments, and tell yourself an alternative story. When you behave differently, they experience you differently, which shifts their ladder too.

**Applied to conversations:** conflict usually happens at the top of the ladder, where each person has constructed their own story and jumped up. Bring people down to gather more data and conflict often resolves. A useful prompt: *"Help me to understand how you see that."*

**The link back to the Johari Window:** helping someone come down their ladder by sharing your thinking creates a larger public arena (it lessens the facade); asking for feedback reduces the blind spot. The result is a more powerful conversation, less riddled with assumptions, and the other person feels genuinely listened to.

> [!example] The manager who asks you to do extra work
> First story: "It's always me, my time isn't valued, my workload isn't respected." Alternative stories after gathering data: she asked others too; you were the best fit; you impressed her last time with your efficiency. Very different feelings result.

> [!tip] Key insight
> "We are all very fit because we are constantly jumping up our ladders, and it's exhausting." Give people permission to gently call it: "Let me share some more data," or "Please help me come down my ladder."

## 2.5 What is Emotional Intelligence?

Emotional intelligence (EQ) is one of the aspects that differentiates average from exceptional leaders. The idea traces to **Charles Darwin (1872)**, was formulated by **Peter Salovey and John Mayer (1990)**, and was popularised by **Daniel Goleman (1995)** in *Emotional Intelligence: Why It Can Matter More Than IQ*. Goleman is sometimes called the forefather of EQ.

**Goleman's four components of emotional intelligence:**

| Component | Focus |
|---|---|
| **Self-awareness** | Recognising your own emotions |
| **Self-management** | Managing your own emotions |
| **Social awareness** | Reading others' emotions and the room |
| **Social skills** | Managing relationships and interactions |

The core message: emotions provide data that help us make rational decisions and behave adaptively. Emotion is not just important but necessary for good decisions, optimal action, problem solving, coping with change and succeeding. To ignore this data is to neglect information available to us.

> [!quote] Daniel Goleman
> In a very real sense we have two minds, one that thinks and one that feels.

> [!example] EQ playing out
> You walk into your manager's office for a conversation you've been longing for and notice her desk is loaded and she looks fatigued and overwhelmed (unlike her usual organised self). Noticing this, and choosing whether to proceed, reschedule or offer help, is EQ in action. The indicators can be subtle: body language, tone, language.

**The top five workplace triggers:**

1. Condescension and lack of respect.
2. Being treated unfairly.
3. Being unappreciated.
4. Feeling that you're not being listened to or heard.
5. Being held to unrealistic deadlines.

**Managing emotions (the Neutral Zone):** between what you were thinking and feeling but not saying, and what was actually said and done, there is a neutral zone where you manage your emotions. This is where the Ladder of Inference and alternative stories do their work.

> [!info] Cross-cultural note
> Being emotionally intelligent in one culture does not necessarily translate across cultures. A smile may mask awkwardness rather than signal happiness, and facial cues are read differently. **Display rules** (cultural norms about amplifying, masking or neutralising emotion) are learned in childhood. In one study, Americans rated expressions of happiness, sadness and surprise more intensely than Japanese participants. East Asian participants read emotion more from the eyes; Western participants from the eyebrows and mouth (reflected even in emoticons).

> [!note] Emotional health and the line of choice (Global Leadership Foundation)
> Emotionally healthy people take personal responsibility for how they respond. The **line of choice** separates automatic, often negative reactions **below the line** (denial, blame, justification, defensiveness) from thoughtful, constructive responses **above the line**. They switch on the **inner observer**: being both "in" an experience and "outside" it at once. **Presence** (whole body thinking) balances three centres of intelligence: head (rational), heart (feeling and intuition) and body (gut instinct).

> [!info] Women and EQ (Korn Ferry / Hay Group study)
> Based on 55,000 professionals across 90 countries (2011 to 2015), women scored higher than men on nearly all EQ competencies except emotional self-control (where there was no difference). Women were **86%** more likely to consistently use emotional self-awareness, **45%** more likely to demonstrate empathy, and **9%** more likely to show a positive outlook.

## 2.6 Difficult Conversations

A difficult conversation is *any conversation you find difficult*: what is easy for you may be difficult for others, and vice versa. A difficult conversation is really **three conversations in one**. We usually assume it is only about what happened, who is right and what to do, but two other conversations are always present.

| Conversation | What it is really about | The shift to make |
|---|---|---|
| **The What Happened Conversation** | We assume we know the truth ("my interpretation is correct, I'm right, you're wrong") | What is important matters more than what is true or who is right. Separate intentions from impact. Move from blame to a **contribution frame**. |
| **The Feelings Conversation** | Feelings are at the heart of difficult conversations | Surface the discrepancy: what was I thinking but not saying? What was I feeling but not saying? Understand, talk about and manage the underlying feelings. |
| **The Identity Conversation** | What you say to yourself about yourself | Our self-image can be knocked off balance. Three identity questions: Am I competent? Am I a good person? Am I worthy of love? These tend to elicit all-or-nothing responses. |

> [!tip] Key insight
> We invent the intentions of others, assume they were bad, and let that assumption drive our behaviour. Separating intention from impact, and shifting from blame to contribution, defuses the conversation.

## 2.7 Engaging in Difficult Conversations

Presented by **Janet Horton (Senior Facilitator and Coach)**. It helps to have a plan thought through ahead of time, without sticking to it rigidly.

**The seven-step process for challenging conversations:**

1. **State the issue** (short, eight words or less, or one sentence).
2. **Acknowledge your part** (settles the defensive reaction so they can hear you).
3. **Share the data** (what you saw, heard, when, the context).
4. **Describe the emotion** (was I upset, concerned? What am I owning?).
5. **Clarify the impact** of that emotion.
6. **Commit to a resolution** ("I'm sure we can find our way through this": gives hope and intent).
7. **Invite a reply** (stop and listen).

**Tips for improving your conversations:**

- Start with yourself (the only person you can change).
- Accept responsibility for your own role or contribution.
- Choose your attitude, approach and mindset.
- Be clear on your intention or purpose.
- Be curious and keen to learn.
- Stay low on the Ladder of Inference.

> [!tip] Key insight
> Acknowledging your own contribution early settles the other person's defensiveness and makes it far more likely they can actually hear what you are trying to say.

## 2.8 Negotiations and Influence

Most of us approach negotiation in "battle armour": how do I get what you won't give, and keep you from getting what I don't want you to have. This creates an adversarial interaction. The material (drawing on **Margaret Neale**, and Neale and Lys, *Getting (More Of) What You Want*, 2015) suggests moving from a **battle mentality to one of fluency**: what am I trying to achieve, and what strategies and tactics will get me there?

**Reframe the goal:** the goal of negotiation is getting a *good* deal, not always getting to "yes". Negotiations are **interdependent**; there is no command and control (not even for a CEO or a parent). A good outcome leaves you **better off than the status quo or your alternatives**, while allowing your counterpart to be whole or better off, because they have to willingly agree.

**Neale's steps:**

| Step | Question |
|---|---|
| **Assess** | Can I change the outcome in a way that makes me better off? |
| **Prepare** | What is it that they want? |
| **Engage** | Bring assessment and preparation together: what is the plan? |
| **Package** | Use "if / then" (yoking) language. |
| **Mindset** | The key to all steps. |

**Three questions to ask before asking:** Why am I asking? How am I asking? For whom am I asking?

**Key practices from the video:**

- **Preparation** is the single most important factor you can control. Most negotiators are woefully underprepared.
- **Listen** to what people tell you is hard or easy for them, rather than only pushing your own perspective.
- **First offer:** making it anchors your counterpart; receiving it gives you an information advantage. Which is more powerful depends on how prepared each party is.
- **Do not solve the easy issues first.** It assumes your easy issues are their easy issues, and leaves the "800lb gorilla" with nothing left to trade.
- **Negotiate at the package level, not issue by issue.** Issue by issue becomes a series of battles where everyone keeps score.
- **Know when to walk away:** understand your alternatives, your bottom line (point of indifference between yes and walking away) and your **aspiration** (an optimistic assessment of what is possible).

> [!quote] Margaret Neale (HBR)
> Just like star athletes who are "in the zone", wise negotiators are centered, energized, and resilient in the face of strong feelings.

> [!tip] Key insight
> Negotiation is about influence, not command and control. Understand your counterpart's interests and what motivates them, and craft a package that makes you better off while leaving them whole or better off.

## 2.10 Peer Coaching Session 3: The GROW Model

Coaching empowers others to increase their capacity. It is *not* about the coach providing answers, solutions or suggestions (that is a big no-no). Stay in the seat of the coach: listen, ask questions, and help the coachee find and leverage their own strengths. The **GROW Model** (adapted from **Whitmore, 2010**) provides a flexible process, not a rigid script. The coachee sets the agenda.

The first half of the conversation is **exploratory and divergent** (Goal and Reality); the second half **converges** towards a meaningful, actionable outcome (Options and Wrap-up).

| Stage | Purpose | Sample questions |
|---|---|---|
| **G - Goal** | Set and clarify the goal (the coaching contract). Frame it as an approach goal (what they want) rather than an avoidance goal (what they want to avoid). | What would you like to achieve? What would need to happen for this time to feel well spent? What would you like to be different when you leave? |
| **R - Reality** | Bring the coachee into the present; test assumptions and surface limiting beliefs. Hold up the mirror respectfully. | What is happening now? What have you tried? What is working? How do you know that is accurate? |
| **O - Options** | Generate as many possibilities as possible, then refine for benefits and pitfalls. | What are your options? What else is possible? Who could help? What resources could you gather? |
| **W - Wrap-up (What Now?)** | Land on next steps and commitment. Listen for the commitment; the coachee must own it. | What are your next steps? What one small step will you take now? When? On a scale of 1 to 10, how committed are you? |

> [!tip] Key insight
> Stay in the seat of the coach. Use divergent, open, curious questions (never leading ones), and let the coachee own the commitment. Ask "what else?" to keep expanding options. The reflective practice ("what worked, what could have been even better if") is the growth opportunity for both coach and coachee.
