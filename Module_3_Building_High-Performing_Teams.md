## Module Overview

This module explores what makes teams effective or dysfunctional, covering team development stages, trust-building, conflict management, decision-making approaches, accountability frameworks, and results measurement. Key frameworks include Tuckman's Stages, Lencioni's Five Dysfunctions, the Thomas-Kilmann Conflict Mode Instrument, and the Balanced Scorecard.

### Key Themes

- Culture is created through individual behaviour choices
- Trust is the foundation of high-performing teams
- Healthy conflict drives better decisions
- Commitment requires clear requests and genuine buy-in
- Accountability must be actively created through conversation
- Results must be collective and visible

## 3.1 The Power of Teams

### Core Concept

What makes teams effective or dysfunctional? Culture is created through conditioning — teams either consciously shape their culture or unconsciously inherit one.

### The Monkeys and the Ladder

A parable illustrating how organisational culture becomes ingrained:

- Four monkeys learn to avoid climbing a ladder (sprinklers punish them)
- New monkeys are introduced one by one
- Eventually, four new monkeys avoid the ladder — but none know why
- **Lesson**: "We've always done it this way" is often unexamined conditioning

### Cross-Cultural Teamwork

Effective global teams require three interconnected practices:

|Practice|Description|Actions|
|---|---|---|
|**Mutual Learning**|Absorbing cultural knowledge from teammates|Listen carefully, observe, ask questions, relate on a personal level|
|**Mutual Understanding**|Grasping WHY people behave as they do, not just WHAT|Suspend judgment, accept ambiguity, let go of assumptions|
|**Mutual Teaching**|Serving as a cultural broker between groups|Notice misinterpretations, help each side see the other's perspective|

### Erin Meyer's Culture Map

Eight scales for understanding cultural differences:

1. **Communicating**: Low-context (explicit) ↔ High-context (implicit)
2. **Evaluating**: Direct negative feedback ↔ Indirect negative feedback
3. **Persuading**: Principles-first ↔ Applications-first
4. **Leading**: Egalitarian ↔ Hierarchical
5. **Deciding**: Consensual ↔ Top-down
6. **Trusting**: Task-based ↔ Relationship-based
7. **Disagreeing**: Confrontational ↔ Avoids confrontation
8. **Scheduling**: Linear-time ↔ Flexible-time

### Women in Leadership & Remote Teams

Research shows female leaders often excel in remote team environments due to strengths in:

- Values-based management
- Emotional intelligence
- Adaptability
- Humanistic leadership styles prioritising trust and flexibility

## 3.2 Culture, Accountability and the Line of Choice

### The Culture Equation

```
Culture = Individual Behaviour × Number of People
```

Every choice you make either lifts the team up or drags it down.

### The Line of Choice Framework

#### ☀️ Above the Line — Personal Responsibility

Taking ownership of outcomes. Focus on solutions, learning, and collective success.

**Behaviours**:

- Ownership
- Accountability
- Solution-focused
- We/Us/Together thinking
- Learning orientation
- Openness

**Typical Questions** (stay above the line):

- "How can I help?"
- "What can we do?"
- "How did this happen?"

#### 🌧️ Below the Line — Defensive Behaviours

Self-protection mode. Destroys trust and stalls progress.

**Behaviours**:

- Defend
- Deny
- Blame
- Justify
- Quit
- Play victim

**Typical Questions** (signal below-the-line thinking):

- "Who did this?"
- "Why did they...?"
- "When did you...?"

### Key Insight

> ⚠️ "I know this is below the line, but..." is itself below the line. Don't use the model as a prefix — use it to check yourself and make a different choice.

## 3.3 Tuckman's Stages of Team Development

### Background

Bruce Tuckman's 1965 model describes five stages teams move through. Mary Ann Jensen added the fifth stage (Adjourning) in 1977. Teams don't progress neatly — they can regress to earlier stages when:

- New members join
- Leadership changes
- Major organisational shifts occur

### The Five Stages

#### Stage 1: Forming — Getting to Know Each Other

**Characteristics**:

- Polite, cautious, overly careful
- Figuring out roles and purpose
- Trust hasn't formed yet
- High learning, low productivity
- Avoiding conflict and controversy

**Leadership Approach**:

- Help members get to know each other
- Clarify purpose and expectations
- Share management expectations
- Give time to bond, but keep things moving

---

#### Stage 2: Storming — Conflict and Power Struggles

**Characteristics**:

- Politeness wears off
- Disagreements surface about mission, process, power
- True personalities emerge
- Competition is high
- Anxiety abounds
- Cliques may form

**Leadership Approach**:

- Don't ignore conflict — acknowledge it as natural
- Surface issues and address them upfront
- Review ground rules
- Revisit purpose

> 💡 Storming is the hardest stage but necessary for healthy team development. When members trust enough to air differences, they're ready to work things out.

---

#### Stage 3: Norming — Establishing Ground Rules

**Characteristics**:

- Team develops "rules of engagement"
- Roles and responsibilities become clear
- Members appreciate each other's skills
- Focus shifts from emotions to processes
- Conflicts get addressed and resolved
- Subgroups may form to move faster

**Leadership Approach**:

- Process is working well; now focus on task delivery
- Be diligent about the roadmap
- Provide time for feedback and closure

---

#### Stage 4: Performing — High Productivity

**Characteristics**:

- Highly effective, problem-solving unit
- Autonomous and self-regulating
- Loyal, with healthy debate
- "We" over "I" orientation
- Problems solved quickly
- Issues anticipated before they become problems
- High trust, openness, and support

**Leadership Approach**:

- Step back — the team handles decisions independently
- Road maps and processes managed by the team

---

#### Stage 5: Adjourning — Wrapping Up

**Characteristics**:

- Project ends or team dissolves
- Activities wrap up
- Outcomes reported
- Information handed over to successors
- Successes celebrated
- Members may feel loss or relief

**Leadership Approach**:

- Track progress to meet deadlines
- Ensure proper handover
- Recognise achievements
- Provide closure

### Tuckman + Line of Choice

A below-the-line culture makes each stage more destructive:

|Stage|Above the Line|Below the Line|
|---|---|---|
|Forming|Healthy exploration|Suspicious, guarded|
|Storming|Productive conflict|Toxic warfare|
|Norming|Good standards|Normalising bad behaviour|
|Performing|High achievement|Never reached|
|Adjourning|Healthy closure|Bitter endings|

### The Four Meetings Red Flag

A sign of below-the-line culture:

1. Corridor talk before the meeting
2. The actual meeting
3. The unspoken meeting in your head
4. The corridor debrief afterwards

**Above-the-line teams have ONE meeting.**

## 3.4 The Dysfunctions of a Team and the Absence of Trust

### Lencioni's Five Dysfunctions Pyramid

Patrick Lencioni's model identifies five interconnected dysfunctions. The pyramid builds from the base — each dysfunction enables the one above it.

```
        ┌─────────────────────────┐
        │  Inattention to Results │  ← Level 5
        ├─────────────────────────┤
        │ Avoidance of            │  ← Level 4
        │ Accountability          │
        ├─────────────────────────┤
        │ Lack of Commitment      │  ← Level 3
        ├─────────────────────────┤
        │ Fear of Conflict        │  ← Level 2
        ├─────────────────────────┤
        │ Absence of Trust        │  ← Level 1 (Foundation)
        └─────────────────────────┘
```

### Level 1: Absence of Trust (Foundation)

**The Dysfunction**: Without trust, nothing else works. Team members are unwilling to be vulnerable, admit mistakes, or ask for help.

**How to Fix**:

- Be vulnerable first as a leader
- Share failures and mistakes
- Walk-talks (ask: "What's one thing I do well? What's one thing I could improve?")
- 360-degree feedback
- Johari Window exercises
- Build personal relationships

---

### Level 2: Fear of Conflict

**The Dysfunction**: Without trust, people won't engage in honest, productive debate. Meetings become boring and superficial.

**How to Fix**:

- Understand conflict styles (TKI)
- Mine for different opinions
- Acknowledge that healthy conflict is productive
- Demand debate on important issues

---

### Level 3: Lack of Commitment

**The Dysfunction**: Without real debate, people don't truly buy in. They may comply publicly but resist privately.

**How to Fix**:

- Use Tell-Sell-Test-Consult-Joint framework
- Clarify decisions and deadlines
- Review commitments regularly
- Ensure clarity even without consensus

---

### Level 4: Avoidance of Accountability

**The Dysfunction**: Without commitment, no one holds anyone responsible. Mediocrity is tolerated.

**How to Fix**:

- Publicise goals and standards
- Regular progress reviews
- Peer accountability
- Commitment and accountability conversations

---

### Level 5: Inattention to Results

**The Dysfunction**: Team members put individual needs (ego, career, recognition) ahead of collective goals.

**How to Fix**:

- Make results public
- Use scoreboards and visible metrics
- Reward team achievements, not just individual performance
- Balanced Scorecard approach

---

### Building Trust

#### Strategies for Building Trust

- **Vulnerability first**: Leaders model by sharing their own weaknesses
- **Walk-talks**: Regular one-on-one conversations asking for feedback
- **360-degree feedback**: Formalised multi-source input
- **Johari Window**: Framework for self-disclosure and seeking feedback
- **Personal histories**: Share backgrounds and experiences
- **Team effectiveness exercises**: Structured feedback sessions

#### Cross-Cultural Trust: Cognitive vs Affective

|Type|Basis|Built Through|Cultures That Prioritise|
|---|---|---|---|
|**Cognitive Trust**|Head (task-based)|Demonstrating competence, reliability, accomplishments|US, Germany, UK, Australia, Netherlands|
|**Affective Trust**|Heart (relationship-based)|Emotional closeness, personal connection, shared meals|China, Brazil, Saudi Arabia, India, Nigeria|

**Key Insight**: In relationship-based cultures, you must build personal connection (e.g., guanxi in China) before business. Meals, socialising, and getting to know each other come first.

## 3.5 Addressing the Fear of Conflict

### Core Concept

Trusting teams engage in **healthy conflict** — open, transparent, robust debate where all issues get put on the table. The goal is the best outcome, not personal victory.

### Thomas-Kilmann Conflict Mode Instrument (TKI)

The TKI maps five conflict-handling styles along two axes:

- **Assertiveness**: Satisfying your own concerns
- **Cooperativeness**: Satisfying others' concerns

```
     High  ┌─────────────┬─────────────┐
           │  COMPETING  │COLLABORATING│
           │  (Win-Lose) │  (Win-Win)  │
Assertive- ├─────────────┼─────────────┤
ness       │       COMPROMISING        │
           │      (Partial Win)        │
           ├─────────────┬─────────────┤
           │  AVOIDING   │ACCOMMODATING│
     Low   │ (Lose-Lose) │ (Lose-Win)  │
           └─────────────┴─────────────┘
              Low    Cooperativeness   High
```

### The Five Conflict Modes

#### 🏆 Competing (Win-Lose)

**Description**: High assertiveness, low cooperativeness. Pursue your concerns at others' expense. Power-oriented.

**Use When**:

- Quick, decisive action is vital (emergencies)
- Unpopular but necessary decisions (cost-cutting, discipline)
- You know you're right on vital issues
- Protecting yourself from exploitation

**Risk of Overuse**:

- Creates "yes people"
- Stifles learning and information flow
- Others afraid to admit uncertainties

---

#### 🤝 Collaborating (Win-Win)

**Description**: High assertiveness, high cooperativeness. Work together to find solutions that fully satisfy both parties.

**Use When**:

- Both parties' concerns are too important to compromise
- Your objective is to learn
- You want to merge insights from different perspectives
- You need genuine commitment
- Working through hard feelings

**Risk of Overuse**:

- Time-consuming
- Not every issue warrants this investment
- Can be exploited by competitive counterparts

---

#### ⚖️ Compromising (Partial Win)

**Description**: Moderate assertiveness and cooperativeness. Find expedient, mutually acceptable middle ground.

**Use When**:

- Goals are moderately important
- Time pressure requires quick resolution
- Equal power and mutually exclusive goals
- Temporary settlement of complex issues
- Backup when collaboration fails

**Risk of Overuse**:

- May lose sight of bigger principles
- Can create cynical gamesmanship
- Splits the difference rather than solving the problem

---

#### 🚶 Avoiding (Lose-Lose)

**Description**: Low assertiveness, low cooperativeness. Sidestep, postpone, or withdraw from the conflict.

**Use When**:

- Issue is trivial
- No chance of satisfying your concerns
- Costs of confrontation outweigh benefits
- People need to cool down
- Others can resolve it more effectively
- Issue is symptomatic of a bigger problem

**Risk of Overuse**:

- Decisions made by default
- Walking on eggshells culture
- Coordination suffers

---

#### 🎁 Accommodating (Lose-Win)

**Description**: Low assertiveness, high cooperativeness. Neglect your own concerns to satisfy others. Self-sacrifice.

**Use When**:

- You realise you're wrong
- Issue matters more to the other person
- Building goodwill for later issues
- You're outmatched and losing
- Preserving harmony is critical
- Helping others learn from mistakes

**Risk of Overuse**:

- Your ideas don't get attention
- Discipline becomes lax
- Lose respect and influence

---

### The Library Book Example

Illustrating how different modes handle the same situation:

|Mode|Response to "I need to keep this book longer"|
|---|---|
|**Competing**|"Rules say return it — there's a fine."|
|**Accommodating**|"Keep it as long as you need."|
|**Avoiding**|"Talk to someone else about that."|
|**Compromising**|"Return it next week instead of tomorrow."|
|**Collaborating**|"We need it back, but here's the ebook, we can photocopy key pages, or reserve another copy for you."|

**Key Insight**: No mode is inherently right or wrong. The skill is matching mode to situation.

## 3.6 Commitment and the Tell-Sell Decision Making Approach

### Core Concept

Teams that don't engage in healthy conflict won't truly commit to decisions. They may comply publicly but resist privately. Real commitment requires clarity and buy-in.

### Tannenbaum-Schmidt Decision-Making Spectrum

```
Leader Authority                              Team Influence
     High ◄─────────────────────────────────────► High

   ┌────────┬────────┬────────┬────────┬────────┐
   │  TELL  │  SELL  │  TEST  │CONSULT │ JOINT  │
   └────────┴────────┴────────┴────────┴────────┘
```

### The Five Decision-Making Styles

|Style|Description|When to Use|
|---|---|---|
|**Tell**|Leader announces the decision|Emergencies, non-negotiables, clear expertise|
|**Sell**|Leader announces decision and explains rationale|Buy-in matters but decision is made|
|**Test**|Leader proposes tentative decision, seeks input|Leader has preference but open to change|
|**Consult**|Leader gathers input, then makes decision|Team expertise needed, building commitment|
|**Joint**|Leader and team decide together by consensus|High-stakes, need full buy-in, diverse expertise|

### Key Principle

All five styles are valid — the dysfunction occurs when:

- People think they're in "consult" but the decision is already made
- Leaders disguise "tell" as something more participative
- The approach isn't transparent

**Be explicit about which approach you're using.**

### Teams That Commit vs Don't Commit

|Committed Teams|Uncommitted Teams|
|---|---|
|Clarity on direction|Ambiguity breeds anxiety|
|Common objectives|Competing agendas|
|Seize opportunities|Miss opportunities|
|Move forward without hesitation|Revisit decisions repeatedly|
|Learn from mistakes|Second-guess constantly|
|Change direction without guilt|Paralysed by doubt|

## 3.7 Learning to Hold One Another Accountable

### Core Concept

You cannot _make_ someone accountable. Accountability stems from a willing commitment made by one person to another. Without clear commitment, there's nothing to hold someone accountable for.

### Accountable vs Unaccountable Teams

|Accountable Teams|Unaccountable Teams|
|---|---|
|Apply peer pressure on poor performance|Breed resentment|
|Resolve problems quickly|Tolerate mediocrity|
|Show mutual respect|Miss deadlines|
|Give open and timely feedback|Leave issues unresolved|
|Focus on solutions|Burden high performers|

### The Commitment Request Formula

A well-formed request has four components:

```
┌─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
│                                                                 │
│   "In order to [CONTEXT/WHY IT MATTERS]...                     │
│                                                                 │
│    I request you [SPECIFIC ACTION]                             │
│                                                                 │
│    by [DATE AND TIME]...                                       │
│                                                                 │
│    Do you commit to this?"                                     │
│                                                                 │
└─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘
```

### Valid Responses to a Request

|Response|Meaning|Status|
|---|---|---|
|"Yes, I commit"|Clear acceptance|✅ Commitment made|
|"No, I do not commit"|Clear decline|❌ No commitment|
|"I'll respond by [time]"|Need to check something first|⏳ Pending|
|"I need clarification on..."|Ambiguity in request|⏳ Pending|
|"I propose instead..."|Counter-offer|⏳ Negotiation|
|"I'll do my best"|No clear commitment|❌ **This is NO**|
|"Maybe"|No clear commitment|❌ **This is NO**|

> ⚠️ **Critical**: "Maybe" and "I'll do my best" count as NO. You cannot hold someone accountable for a commitment they didn't make.

### The Accountability Conversation (7 Steps)

When a commitment is broken, use this structured conversation:

#### Step 1: Check Your Intention

_Before the conversation_. Manage your emotions. Clarify what you want to achieve. If frustrated or angry, your conversation will express dissatisfaction rather than create learning.

**Useful intentions**:

- Help the person understand why this matters
- Help them learn
- Let them know how it affects you
- Seek a commitment to resolve outstanding issues

#### Step 2: Set the Scene

Establish the context for the conversation.

#### Step 3: Assert and Check Prior Commitment

Confirm you're both on the same page about what was agreed.

#### Step 4: Assert and Check Breakdown

State clearly that the commitment was not met.

_Steps 2-4 can often be combined_:

> "Do you recall on Monday I asked you to prepare the report for Friday's board meeting? You agreed to have it to me by 3pm today. It's past 3pm and I don't have the report."

#### Step 5: Inquire About Their Perspective

Ask for their view. They may have:

- Different recollection
- Reasons for not delivering
- Information you don't have

**Distinguish**:

- **Reasonable reasons**: Sound plausible
- **Acceptable reasons**: Genuinely outside their control

Examples:

|Reason|Type|Why|
|---|---|---|
|"Something urgent came up"|Reasonable but NOT acceptable|They could have said no or renegotiated|
|"Sam didn't get me the info"|Reasonable but NOT acceptable|They could have negotiated with Sam or found someone else|
|"Finance system was offline for 8 hours"|Acceptable|Genuinely outside their control|
|"My child had an accident"|Acceptable|Emergency outside their control|

#### Step 6: Issue a Damage Report

State the impact and consequences. Can include:

- **Work impact**: "We're not prepared for the board meeting"
- **Personal feelings**: "I'm disappointed because I was counting on you"
- **Relationship impact**: "This affects my trust in future commitments"

Share your feelings constructively. You're teaching them how this affects you and the organisation.

#### Step 7: Make a New Request

Another commitment conversation. Ask them to make things right or take specific action.

### Creating Non-Accountability (What NOT to Do)

- Ignoring it and saying nothing
- Doing it yourself without addressing it
- "No problem, get to it when you can"
- "OK, that's all right"

These responses teach that accountability doesn't matter.

### Unconscious Bias and Accountability

Biases that distort performance evaluation:

- **In-group favouritism**: Preferring people like us
- **Halo effect**: One positive trait colours everything
- **Confirmation bias**: Seeking evidence that confirms existing beliefs
- **Bandwagon effect**: Following others' opinions

Awareness is a start, but organisations need structured processes to mitigate bias.

## 3.8 The Achievement of Collective Results

### Core Concept

The top of Lencioni's pyramid is results. All the work on trust, conflict, commitment, and accountability must ultimately drive **collective** outcomes — not individual agendas.

### The Balanced Scorecard (Kaplan & Norton)

A framework for measuring results across four perspectives, all anchored to a shared vision.

```
                    ┌─────────────────────┐
                    │      VISION         │
                    │  What are we trying │
                    │    to achieve?      │
                    └─────────┬───────────┘
                              │
        ┌─────────────────────┼─────────────────────┐
        │                     │                     │
        ▼                     ▼                     ▼
┌───────────────┐    ┌───────────────┐    ┌───────────────┐
│   FINANCIAL   │    │   CUSTOMER    │    │   INTERNAL    │
│               │    │               │    │   PROCESS     │
│ How should we │    │ How should we │    │               │
│ appear to     │    │ appear to     │    │ What must we  │
│ stakeholders? │    │ customers?    │    │ excel at?     │
└───────────────┘    └───────────────┘    └───────────────┘
                              │
                              ▼
                    ┌───────────────────┐
                    │ LEARNING & GROWTH │
                    │                   │
                    │ How will we       │
                    │ sustain ability   │
                    │ to change?        │
                    └───────────────────┘
```

### The Four Perspectives

|Perspective|Key Question|Focus Areas|
|---|---|---|
|**Financial**|How should we appear to stakeholders?|Revenue, costs, profitability, ROI, budget adherence|
|**Customer**|How should we appear to customers?|Satisfaction, retention, market share, value proposition|
|**Internal Process**|What processes must we excel at?|Quality, efficiency, innovation, operational excellence|
|**Learning & Growth**|How will we sustain our ability to change?|Skills, culture, technology, leadership development|

### Lead vs Lag Indicators

- **Lead indicators**: Drive future results (predictive)
- **Lag indicators**: Measure past results (outcomes)

Typically, one perspective serves as the lead indicator driving the others.

### Making Results Visible

- Public scoreboards
- Shared metrics
- Team-level (not just individual) rewards
- Regular progress reviews

Visibility reinforces accountability and keeps focus on collective achievement.
