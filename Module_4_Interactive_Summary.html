4.1
Problem Framing
How we define a problem shapes every decision that follows. Framing too narrowly — or in terms of a solution — limits the possibilities we can see.
The Core Principle Foundational
Frame defines the entire solution space.
Frame at the right level of abstraction — not in terms of a solution
❌ "I need to buy a car" → ✅ "I need to address my transportation needs"
Be mindful of your own biases and involve people with different perspectives
Good framing provides clarity, narrows scope, achieves alignment, and increases efficiency

Sound Decision Making Principles
Good decisions don't guarantee good outcomes.
Decision making is often the final step in problem solving
Better decisions usually lead to better outcomes
We can't always know the best decision — but we can make a good one
We are subject to many factors that influence the quality of our decisions

6 Problem Solving Traps Watch Out
Common traps that distort how we frame and solve problems.
Overconfidence — overestimating the accuracy of your own judgement
Confirmation bias — seeking evidence that supports existing beliefs
Ignoring new information — dismissing data that challenges your frame
Short-term focus — prioritising present needs over future ones
Self-interest — allowing personal gain to distort analysis
Mental models — unconscious assumptions that constrain thinking

How do different people in your team perceive the problem?
Different people perceive the same problem differently — this is a resource, not a problem
As a leader, surface multiple frames before locking in a definition
Stakeholder conversations often reveal that your initial frame was incomplete or biased
Ask: "Is my PLP framed as a problem — or as a solution?"

4.2
Strategic Thinking Skills
Stepping back from day-to-day reactive work to analyse the broader environment and organisational realities. Five skills (Hughes & Colarelli Beatty) and two scanning tools.
The 5 Strategic Thinking Skills Hughes & Beatty
Scanning · Visioning · Reframing · Making Meaning · Thinking Systemically
Scanning — Analysing the current environment; looking outward at competitors, markets, technology
Visioning — Communicating a compelling picture of what the organisation can become
Reframing — Questioning beliefs and assumptions; seeing problems as potential opportunities
Making meaning — Translating vision into meaningful day-to-day decisions people can apply
Thinking systemically — Looking at interrelationships, structures, and mental models to find high-leverage interventions

SWOT Analysis Tool
Strengths · Weaknesses · Opportunities · Threats
Strengths — Internal: what the organisation does well
Weaknesses — Internal: requires honesty and courage to surface
Opportunities — External: technology, market changes, new possibilities
Threats — External: risks in the market that could impact the organisation
Key question: how can strengths counter weaknesses and threats? How can threats become opportunities?

STEEPLED Analysis Tool
A broader scan of the external environment across 8 dimensions.
Social · Technological · Economic · Environmental
Political · Legal · Ethical · Demographic
Use together with SWOT — STEEPLED stretches scanning beyond internal organisational concerns
Diverse perspectives improve the quality of the analysis — invite others in

The 3–5 pivot points that drive the entire strategy.
Analysis narrows to 3–5 critical factors for success that drive the whole strategy
Everything the organisation invests in is shaped by these drivers
Example: differentiating on quality (high price, low volume) vs. price (low quality, high volume)
Example: if R&D is the key driver, attracting specialist talent becomes the strategic priority

4.3
The Systems Thinking Iceberg
Most organisations react to events. Leaders who think systemically work below the waterline — addressing patterns, structures, and mental models to produce lasting change.

Click each level to explore what lies beneath the surface

▲ EVENTS
What just happened?
React
Visible incidents — a client complaint, a missed deadline, something breaking down. We react and fix the immediate problem. While necessary, continually reacting to events prevents strategic action. These are the newspaper headlines — anyone can see them. They sit above the waterline.
· · · waterline · · ·
PATTERNS
What has been happening?
Anticipate
When enough events are observed over time, patterns and trends emerge. Working at this level allows leaders to anticipate, predict, or forecast rather than simply react. Our interpretations and thinking occur here — and they aren't always visible until we make them so.
STRUCTURE
Why is this happening?
Change
The organisational systems that produce patterns: hierarchy, sign-off processes, physical design, policies, reward mechanisms, decision-making criteria, information access, and what leadership pays attention to (the unspoken rules of the game). Structure drives behaviour — if you want people to act differently, change the structure.
▼ MENTAL MODELS
How does our thinking shape the situation?
Transform
Deeply held internal images of how the world works — the assumptions and beliefs that drive the structures we create. Peter Senge: "Mental models are deeply held internal images of how the world works, images that limit us to familiar ways of thinking and acting." Surfacing and testing mental models produces the most profound and lasting change.
Key message: Our mental models drive our decisions and the structures we create. Being open to exploring, surfacing, and testing mental models is crucial for significant and lasting change in our organisations.
4.4
Rational Decision Making
A methodical six-step model for well-defined problems, three pre-decision steps, and an awareness that cultural background shapes how decisions are made and implemented.

The Six-Step Model (Bazerman & Moore, 2012)

1
Define the problem
▼
Articulate the problem clearly before jumping to solutions. A well-defined problem is half solved. Avoid framing in terms of a preferred solution.
2
Identify the criteria
▼
What factors matter in making this decision? List all relevant criteria — cost, risk, feasibility, stakeholder impact, time, alignment with values.
3
Assess the criteria
▼
Weigh the criteria relative to each other. Not all criteria are equal — some matter more than others in this specific context.
4
Generate alternatives
▼
Identify all realistic options before evaluating any of them. Avoid anchoring too early on one solution. More options = better decision quality.
5
Rate each alternative on each criterion
▼
Systematically evaluate each option against every criterion identified. A simple scoring matrix makes this process transparent and defensible.
6
Compute the optimal decision
▼
Combine ratings and weights to arrive at the highest-scoring option. Note: this model works best for well-defined problems — not all situations suit this level of analysis.

3 Pre-Decision Steps (Matthew Confer)

1. Challenge the Constraints
What barriers are actually fixed?
Too many people accept all constraints as given — start by questioning them
Stanford $5 team: ignored the seed money entirely and sold their presentation slot — the real asset was the audience of students
Constraints prevent innovative ideas from ever developing
click to expand
2. Embrace a Premortem
How could this fail before we begin?
Flip the postmortem: contemplate failure before going down a path
The Cobra Effect: British government paid bounties for dead cobras → people bred cobras → program cancelled → breeders released snakes → worse problem than before
A premortem would have identified this unintended consequence
click to expand
3. Check the Basics
Don't let small details be the downfall.
Even brilliant strategic plans fail on overlooked details
NASA's 1999 Mars Climate Orbiter: one team used metric, another used imperial — $125 million lost to a unit-of-measurement error
Under pressure, teams struggle to complete the most basic tasks — check them deliberately

Cultural Dimensions of Decision Making (Erin Meyer)

Unilateral e.g. American

🔵 Decisions made quickly, often by one person
🔵 Flexible and revisable as new info emerges
🔵 Implementation can take longer — not everyone aligned
🔵 A decision with a lowercase d

Consensual e.g. Swedish, Japanese

🟣 Decision-making takes longer — everyone consulted
🟣 Once made, the decision is fixed
🟣 Implementation is rapid — everyone already on board
🟣 A decision with a capital D (e.g. Japanese ringi)
Tip for multicultural teams: Explicitly discuss and agree upon a decision-making method early in any collaboration. Define whether it's by vote or by the leader, whether 100% agreement is needed, and how open the group will be to later changes.

Cultural Brokerage & Creative Performance
Jang, S. (2017). INSEAD Working Paper. How multicultural team members leverage diverse knowledge to produce creative outcomes.
Key Definitions Concepts
Cultural brokerage, insiders, outsiders, integrating, eliciting.
Cultural brokerage: facilitating interactions across cultural boundaries
Cultural insider: multicultural whose background overlaps with other team members
Cultural outsider: multicultural whose background has no overlap with other team members
Integrating: synthesising knowledge from different cultures into a novel whole
Eliciting: inquiring about and drawing out relevant cultural knowledge from others
click to expand
Key Findings Results
What both studies found about multicultural teams and creativity.
Both cultural insiders and outsiders enhanced team creative performance
Insiders tend to broker by integrating; outsiders tend to broker by eliciting
Both integrating and eliciting — when enacted by multiculturals — significantly improved team creativity
Integrating and eliciting together explained 28% of the variance in team creative performance
Monocultural members who attempted to elicit actually decreased team creative performance
Cultural outsiders are as valuable as insiders — despite being consistently overlooked
click to expand
Leadership Implication Apply
What this means for how you lead diverse teams.
Understand who the cultural brokers are in your team — and create conditions for them to play that role
Both cultural insiders and outsiders contribute meaningfully — don't overlook the outsiders
Don't expect monocultural members to bridge cultural divides — this role belongs to the multiculturals
Formal role assignment (appointing someone as cultural broker) can help legitimise brokerage behaviour
