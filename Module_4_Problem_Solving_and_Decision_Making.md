## Module Overview

This module develops the capabilities needed to identify, frame, and analyse complex problems and make sound decisions. As leaders assume more responsibility, the problems they face become more complex and less amenable to simple, technical solutions. Key frameworks include the Systems Thinking Iceberg, the Rational Decision Making Model, and the d.school Design Thinking Process.

### Key Themes

- How we frame a problem determines how we go about solving it
- Better decisions usually lead to better outcomes — but good decisions don't guarantee good outcomes
- Structure drives behaviour: if you want people to act differently, change the system
- Mental models are often invisible — surfacing them is essential for lasting change
- Design thinking is a human-centred, iterative process, not a linear one
- Cultural background shapes how people perceive problems and make decisions

## 4.1 Problem Framing Refresher

### Core Concept

How we define (or frame) a problem influences the way we go about solving it. Problem framing is the process of describing and interpreting a problem to arrive at a problem statement — an essential step before any solution design begins.

### Key Principles of Problem Framing

- Frame at the correct level of abstraction — be careful not to frame in terms of a solution
  - ❌ "I need to buy a car"
  - ✅ "I need to address my transportation needs"
- Be mindful of your own biases and involve others with different perspectives
- Framing provides clarity, narrows scope, achieves alignment, and increases efficiency
- It helps avoid wasted time and resources on irrelevant or superficial solutions

### Principles of Sound Decision Making

A good decision does not necessarily lead to a good outcome — the best information at the time may still not be enough to predict results.

- Decision making is often the final step in solving a problem
- Better decisions usually lead to better outcomes
- It is not always possible to know the best decision, but we can exercise judgement to make informed ones
- Decision makers are subject to a wide range of factors that influence the quality of their decisions

### Common Problem Solving Traps

Leaders must recognise and guard against the following:

| Trap | Description |
| --- | --- |
| **Overconfidence** | Overestimating the accuracy of your own judgement |
| **Confirmation bias** | Seeking evidence that supports existing beliefs |
| **Ignoring new information** | Dismissing data that challenges your frame |
| **Short-term focus** | Focusing on present needs rather than future ones |
| **Self-interest** | Allowing personal gain to distort analysis |
| **Mental models** | Unconscious assumptions that constrain thinking |

> 💡 **Ask yourself**: As a leader, how will you capitalise on the different ways in which individuals perceive a problem?

## 4.2 Strategic Thinking Skills

### Core Concept

Strategic thinking requires stepping back from day-to-day reactive work to analyse the broader environment and organisational realities. These skills can be learned and developed.

### The Five Strategic Thinking Skills (Hughes & Colarelli Beatty)

| Skill | Description |
| --- | --- |
| **Scanning** | Analysing the current environment and organisational realities — looking outward |
| **Visioning** | Communicating a compelling picture of what the organisation can become |
| **Reframing** | Questioning beliefs and assumptions; seeing problems as potential opportunities |
| **Making meaning** | Translating vision and mission into meaningful day-to-day decisions for people |
| **Thinking systemically** | Looking at interrelationships, structures, and mental models to find high-leverage interventions |

### SWOT Analysis

A tried-and-tested tool for assessing the environment and identifying areas requiring attention:

| Component | Focus | Questions to Ask |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Strengths** | Internal — what we do well | What do we do best? What's unique about us? |
| **Weaknesses** | Internal — what we don't do well | What can be improved? Where do we underperform? |
| **Opportunities** | External — favourable conditions | What market gaps exist? What are our goals? |
| **Threats** | External — risks and challenges | What trends threaten us? Where do competitors outperform? |

> ⚠️ Identifying weaknesses requires honesty and courage. Create a safe environment for this part of the exercise.

### STEEPLED Analysis

A broader scanning framework to stretch thinking beyond the organisation:

- **S** — Social: values, norms, and societal changes
- **T** — Technological: impact of new technologies on systems, processes, and connectivity
- **E** — Economic: local and global economic conditions and their impact
- **E** — Environmental: biological and environmental factors, including climate
- **P** — Political: how the political landscape shapes policy and legislation
- **L** — Legal: ramifications of employment and other relevant law
- **E** — Ethical: moral considerations affecting decisions
- **D** — Demographic: changes in population structure, immigration, and market composition

### Key Strategic Drivers

The SWOT and STEEPLED analyses narrow to identify the **critical factors for success** — usually three to five pivot points that drive the entire strategy. Everything the organisation invests in is shaped by these drivers.

**Examples**:
- Differentiating on quality (high price, low volume) vs. price (low quality, high volume)
- If R&D is the key driver, attracting and retaining specialist talent becomes the strategic priority

## 4.3 The Systems Thinking Iceberg

### Core Concept

The iceberg model is a framework for systems thinking that illustrates the different levels at which problems can be understood and addressed. What is visible above the surface is often much smaller than what lies beneath.

```
                    ┌──────────────────────────────┐
                    │           EVENTS             │  ← Visible
                    │    "What just happened?"     │
                    │         → React              │
                    └──────────────────────────────┘
- - - - - - - - - - - - - WATERLINE - - - - - - - - - - - - -
                    ┌──────────────────────────────┐
                    │          PATTERNS            │
                    │  "What has been happening?"  │
                    │    → Anticipate, forecast    │
                    └──────────────────────────────┘
                    ┌──────────────────────────────┐
                    │         STRUCTURE            │
                    │   "Why is this happening?"   │
                    │    → Understand, change      │
                    └──────────────────────────────┘
                    ┌──────────────────────────────┐
                    │       MENTAL MODELS          │
                    │  "How does our thinking      │  ← Invisible
                    │   influence the situation?"  │
                    │        → Transform           │
                    └──────────────────────────────┘
```

### The Four Levels

#### 🔹 Events (above the waterline)

Visible incidents — a client complaint, a deadline missed, something breaking down.

- **Question**: What just happened?
- **Response**: React and fix the immediate problem
- **Limitation**: Continually reacting to events prevents strategic action

#### 🔹 Patterns (just below the waterline)

When enough events are observed over time, patterns or trends become visible.

- **Question**: What has been happening?
- **Response**: Anticipate, predict, or forecast
- **Advantage**: Moves beyond pure reaction to preparation

#### 🔹 Structure (deeper below the waterline)

The organisational systems that produce patterns. Includes:

- Hierarchy and sign-off processes
- Physical design and layout
- Policies, procedures, rules, and regulations
- Reward mechanisms — what is being recognised and incentivised?
- Decision-making criteria and who makes decisions
- Information access — who knows what?
- What leadership pays attention to (the unspoken rules of the game)

> **Key message**: Structure drives behaviour. If you want people to behave differently, change the structure.

- **Question**: Why is this happening?
- **Response**: Understand the system and change it

#### 🔹 Mental Models (deepest level)

The deeply held internal images of how the world works — the assumptions and beliefs that drive the structures we create.

> *"Mental models are deeply held internal images of how the world works, images that limit us to familiar ways of thinking and acting. Very often, we are not consciously aware of our mental models or the effects they have on our behavior."*
> — Peter Senge

- **Question**: How does our thinking influence the situation?
- **Response**: Surface, explore, and test mental models to transform the system
- Mental models drive decision making and the structures we create
- Being open to questioning mental models is crucial for significant and lasting change

### The Power of Thinking Below the Waterline

| Level | What You Can Do |
| --- | --- |
| Events | React — address symptoms |
| Patterns | Anticipate — forecast and prepare |
| Structure | Change — alter system behaviour |
| Mental Models | Transform — produce lasting change |

> 💡 **Ask yourself**: Think of a recent event you needed to respond to as a leader. What patterns, structural elements, and mental models underlie that event? What needs to be anticipated, changed, or transformed within that system?

## 4.4 Rational Decision Making Process

### Core Concept

A methodical decision-making process for making more rational decisions (Bazerman & Moore, 2012). Note: this model is not suitable for all situations — particularly multi-stakeholder or highly complex problems — but is very useful for well-defined problems.

### The Six-Step Model

```
Step 1 → Define the problem
Step 2 → Identify the criteria
Step 3 → Assess the criteria
Step 4 → Generate alternatives
Step 5 → Rate each alternative on each criterion
Step 6 → Compute the optimal decision
```

### Three Steps to Better Decision Making (Matthew Confer)

Before making a decision, three pre-decision steps improve outcomes:

#### Step 1: Challenge the Constraints

Too many people jump straight into solving a problem and accept all the constraints presented to them. Begin by considering what barriers are holding you back.

> **Example**: In the Stanford $5 exercise, the winning team ignored the $5 entirely and sold their presentation slot to a company seeking to recruit students — recognising the real asset was the audience, not the seed money. Constraints prevent innovative ideas from ever developing.

#### Step 2: Embrace a Premortem

Rather than conducting a postmortem after failure, invest time upfront contemplating how an idea could fail before pursuing it.

> **Example**: The British government in India offered a bounty for dead cobras. People began breeding cobras for profit. When the program was cancelled, breeders released their snakes — making the problem worse. This is the **cobra effect**: the apparent solution made the situation worse. A premortem would have identified this unintended consequence.

#### Step 3: Check the Basics

Even the most sophisticated strategic plans can be undone by small details.

> **Example**: The 1999 NASA Mars Climate Orbiter was lost because one team used metric measurements and another used imperial — a $125 million error caused by failure to check basics.

### Cultural Dimensions of Decision Making

Decision-making styles vary significantly across cultures. Two key approaches:

| Style | Characteristics | Example Cultures |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Unilateral** | Decisions made quickly by one person; flexible and revisable; implementation can take longer | American |
| **Consensual** | Decision-making takes longer with full consultation; once made, decisions are fixed; implementation is rapid | Swedish, Japanese |

> 💡 Think of unilateral decisions as decisions with a lowercase *d* — flexible and revisable. Consensual decisions are decisions with a capital *D* — fixed once made.

**The Japanese ringi process** is an example of consensual decision making, supported by *nemawashi* — the informal consensus-building that occurs before a formal proposal is made. Literally meaning "root-binding," nemawashi protects an organisation from damage caused by disagreement or lack of commitment.

**Strategies when working across decision-making cultures**:

- Explicitly discuss and agree upon a decision-making method early in any collaboration
- Define parameters: vote or boss decision? Is 100% agreement needed? How open is the group to later changes?
- The more people from different cultural backgrounds talk to each other, the more natural adjustment becomes

> 💡 **Ask yourself**: How can I improve my decision-making skills? What decision-making style does my team default to — and is that always appropriate?

## 4.5 Design Thinking

### Core Concept

Design thinking is a human-centred, non-linear, iterative process for solving complex and ill-defined problems. Based on the work of the Hasso Plattner Institute of Design at Stanford (the d.school), it focuses on understanding user needs, challenging assumptions, and developing and testing innovative solutions.

> *"Design thinking is a human-centered approach to innovation that draws from the designer's toolkit to integrate the needs of people, the possibilities of technology, and the requirements for business success."*
> — Tim Brown, CEO of IDEO

The problem-solving process is a **journey of divergence and convergence** — expanding thinking to explore possibilities, then converging to select the most promising solutions. This cycle repeats throughout the stages.

### The Five Stages of Design Thinking

```
Empathise → Define → Ideate → Prototype → Test
              ↑___________________________|
                     ITERATE
```

#### Stage 1: Empathise

- Who is my user? What matters to them?
- Observe, engage, watch, and listen
- Develop a deep understanding of user needs and motivations
- Set aside your own assumptions to gain real insight
- **Empathy allows design thinkers to set aside their own assumptions about the world**

#### Stage 2: Define

- Unpack and synthesise what you have learned in the Empathise stage
- Define a meaningful and actionable problem statement
- Frame the problem from the **user's** perspective, not the organisation's
  - ❌ "We need to increase market share among teenage girls by 5%"
  - ✅ "Teenage girls need to eat nutritious food in order to thrive, be healthy and grow"
- Identify needs and capture insights
- Begins to lead into the Ideate phase: **"How might we...?"**

#### Stage 3: Ideate

- Generate new ideas — divergent, wild, and creative thinking is encouraged
- Use brainstorming, Brainwriting, Nominal Group Technique, or Worst Possible Idea techniques
- Defer judgement — explore as many ideas as possible before evaluating
- "Considered selection" of the most promising ideas follows (e.g. voting)

#### Stage 4: Prototype

- Iteratively experiment — start building
- Do not get attached to any one prototype
- Know what question you are answering with your prototype
- Inexpensive, scaled-down versions help identify the best solution
- By end of this stage: better understanding of product limitations and real user behaviour

#### Stage 5: Test

- Collate feedback and generate new insights
- Show don't tell — create experiences for users
- Ask users to compare options
- Results often loop back to earlier stages to redefine the problem or refine the solution

> ⚠️ **The five stages are not always sequential.** They do not have to follow a specific order and can occur in parallel or be repeated iteratively. They are different *modes* that contribute to the entire design project, not a fixed linear sequence.

### Design Thinking Mindsets

Without the right mindset, the process cannot work effectively:

| Mindset | Description |
| --- | --- |
| **Be empathetic** | Place yourself in the user's shoes; understand needs and motivations |
| **Be collaborative** | Several minds are stronger than one |
| **Be optimistic** | Believe change is possible, regardless of constraints |
| **Embrace ambiguity** | Get comfortable with complexity and experimentation |
| **Be curious** | Stay open to different ideas; recognise you are not the user |
| **Reframe** | Challenge assumptions; don't take problems at face value |
| **Embrace diversity** | Work with people of different backgrounds and perspectives |
| **Make tangible** | Give ideas physical form to help the team align quickly |
| **Take action** | Run experiments and learn from them |

### Applying Design Thinking as a Leader

Key questions for leaders:

- What are the most important shifts in mindset you need to adopt in order to use this process?
- Which aspect of the design thinking process might help with your Professional Leadership Project (PLP)?
- What is your role as a leader in facilitating this process for your team?

## Related Research: Cultural Brokerage in Multicultural Teams

**Source**: Jang, S. (2017). *Cultural Brokerage and Creative Performance in Multicultural Teams*. INSEAD Working Paper 2017/70/OBH.

This research is directly relevant to the problem solving and decision making themes of this module, particularly in relation to diverse teams and creative performance.

### Key Definitions

- **Cultural brokerage**: The act of facilitating interactions between actors across cultural boundaries
- **Cultural insider**: A multicultural member whose cultural background overlaps with other team members
- **Cultural outsider**: A multicultural member whose cultural background has no overlap with other team members
- **Integrating**: Synthesising and combining knowledge from different cultures into a novel whole
- **Eliciting**: Inquiring about and drawing out relevant cultural knowledge from team members

### Key Findings

| Finding | Detail |
| --- | --- |
| Multiculturals enhance team creativity | Both cultural insiders and outsiders improve creative performance in diverse teams |
| Insiders broker by integrating | Cultural insiders combine and synthesise knowledge across cultures |
| Outsiders broker by eliciting | Cultural outsiders draw out and share relevant knowledge from team members |
| Both types improve outcomes | Integrating and eliciting each significantly enhance team creative performance |
| Cultural outsiders are undervalued | They contribute as meaningfully as insiders, despite often being overlooked |
| Monoculturals should not elicit | Monocultural members who attempt to elicit can actually *decrease* team creative performance |

### Two Studies

- **Study 1 (Archival)**: 2,117 multicultural teams from a global student collaboration project across 40+ countries. Found that both cultural insiders and outsiders enhanced team creative performance.
- **Study 2 (Experimental)**: 83 teams (249 individuals) working on a multicultural wedding planning task. Confirmed brokerage behaviours and found that integrating and eliciting together accounted for **28% of the variance** in team creative performance.

### Leadership Implication

> For leaders managing diverse teams, understanding who the cultural brokers are — and creating conditions for both cultural insiders and outsiders to play that role — can significantly improve problem solving and creative outcomes. This connects directly to the design thinking emphasis on empathy, diverse perspectives, and collaborative ideation.
