# Module 5: Leading Innovation and Change
*WLA Executive Ready Program*

---

## Module Overview

This module focuses on the leadership skills involved in driving innovation and change. It critically examines how innovation and change are inextricably intertwined, explores leadership challenges during times of crisis, and builds understanding of how leaders can foster agile and resilient organisations. It also covers how to engage others in change, deal with resistance, and manage stakeholders to best enable them to support change initiatives.

### Learning Intentions

- Foster and lead creativity and innovation in your organisation
- Understand the barriers to innovation and change
- Learn new strategies for leading change
- Apply creative solutions and innovative methods to current challenges at your organisation

## 5.1 Creativity and Innovation

### Key Distinction

**Creativity** and **innovation** are two separate but related concepts:

- **Creativity** — the conceptualisation of something entirely new; the emergence of an idea from its inception. The ideas are entirely original.
- **Innovation** — taking established ideas and doing something new with them. Either the process is new, the enactment is new, or the reconceptualisation of an original process is new.

> Creativity is not innovation. Innovation is taking ideas that have been established by other people and doing them in a new way. *(Anna Dabrowski)*

Innovation is therefore less daunting than creativity — it is not about producing something brand new from nothing, but about doing something differently to what has been seen before.

### Culture and Creativity

Research (Shao et al., 2019, *Frontiers in Psychology*) identifies three key ways culture shapes creativity:

1. **Different conceptions of creativity** — Western cultures tend to emphasise process- and product-based creativity (novelty, originality, problem-solving). Eastern cultures tend to emphasise person-based creativity, social contribution, and the rediscovery or reinterpretation of tradition.
2. **Different creative processes** — Western/individualist cultures favour novelty and radical creativity; Eastern/collectivist cultures favour usefulness, incremental creativity, and cautious processing.
3. **Different assessment of creativity** — Culturally appropriate measures are essential; Western-developed tools may disadvantage Eastern participants. Results can reflect cultural familiarity rather than true creative capacity.

### Brainstorming Across Cultures

Research by Livermore (HBR, 2016) based on 15 years of study across 55,000 professionals in 98 countries identifies key challenges for multicultural brainstorming teams:

- **The first idea wins** — the most assertive, often most powerful participant sets the direction; conformity crowds out diverse perspectives
- **Language fluency** — non-native speakers must translate, formulate, and re-translate, often missing the moment
- **Saving face** — participants from collectivist cultures (e.g. Asia, Latin America) find it counterintuitive to publicly advocate individual ideas against norms of harmony and conformity
- **Big picture vs. detailed analysis** — cultural differences in approach (e.g. North Americans favour blue-sky thinking; many Germans favour detailed analysis)

**Key finding:** Diverse teams with *low* Cultural Intelligence (CQ) produce *less* innovative ideas than homogeneous teams. High-CQ team members speak up as much or more on diverse teams as on homogeneous ones.

#### Strategies for Cross-Cultural Brainstorming

1. **Clearly define the objective** — ask each participant to write down their understanding of the goal beforehand and align before proceeding
2. **Give advance warning** — don't spring sessions on the team; allow preparation time, and offer alternative ways to contribute (e.g. written input)
3. **Insist on 100% participation** — make this explicit, and offer multiple formats for contribution (verbal, written, collective)

### Innovation and Women

Women contribute distinctive value to innovation through:
- Reimagining existing things
- Capturing emerging trends
- Challenging the status quo (often simply by being in the room)
- Building and sustaining active networks

Despite making up 50% of the population, women hold only 7.5% of patents — highlighting a significant gap in recognised innovation contribution.

## 5.2 Creativity and Constraints

### External Barriers

- Organisational culture — norms, values, and structures that resist change
- Time and resource constraints
- Lack of shared buy-in from colleagues and leaders
- Change fatigue within the organisation

### Internal Barriers

- **Growth mindset** — whether individuals believe they have the capacity to be creative
- **Self-control and desire** — intrinsic motivation to pursue innovation
- **Conflict aversion** — the potential for interpersonal conflict that comes with advocating for new ideas
- Personal beliefs and assumptions about one's own role and power

> We are constrained by our context, our organisational culture, and the resources we are afforded. *(Anna Dabrowski)*

## 5.3 When and Where Do We Get Ideas for Innovation?

### Timing Matters

- There is a **right time** for innovation — entering an organisation and attempting to change everything will generate resistance
- Avoid periods of **change fatigue** — if the organisation is already undergoing significant change, staff may not have the capacity or interest to embrace further innovation
- Incremental innovation is more likely to succeed than radical change

### Where Innovation Happens

- Innovation requires **shared ownership** — mobilising those around you to become invested in the idea
- Those without formal power can still be innovative by **building influence** and engaging others
- Getting stakeholders on board creates a shared voice that supports enactment

### Ask Yourself

- What is ripe for innovation in my organisation?
- When and where will I create space and time for creative thinking around this?

## 5.4 Getting Started on an Innovative Idea

### Process for Getting Started (Dabrowski)

1. **Take a helicopter/balcony view** — step back and look holistically at the organisation: its culture, current changes, its people
2. **Create an opportunity list** — identify areas that could be enhanced or improved
3. **Refine the list** — decide which components to move forward with and which to set aside
4. **Anticipate challenges** — consider what mindsets may need to shift; whose perspectives may not align with the idea
5. **Choose the right time** — consult widely (both influential and non-influential stakeholders); seek shared ownership
6. **Move incrementally, not radically** — incremental change increases the chances of success

### Reflection Prompts

- What is one way that a current opportunity could be realised?
- Who needs to be consulted or brought along?

## 5.5 Dealing with Resistance to Change

### Why Resistance Occurs

- **Sense of loss** — loss of familiar culture, role identity, or ways of working
- **Lack of willingness** — individuals who have operated in the same way for years may find new processes difficult
- **Lack of transparency** — resistance often stems from unclear communication or absence of shared vision
- **Lack of skill** — people may feel they don't have the capabilities required by the proposed change

### Jason Clarke's Framework: Real Reasons People Resist Change

Surface-level objections ("it's always been done this way"; "it's not in the budget") are not the real reasons. The real reasons include:

1. Emotional overwhelm — too much fear or shock to engage
2. Fear of the transition, not the idea itself
3. Uncertainty about how big the change really is
4. Not seeing how they fit into the change
5. Feeling they have no say
6. Scepticism born from past experiences of *fake change*

### The Four Doors of Change

A framework for helping people understand what change means for them:

| Door | Description | Response |
|------|-------------|----------|
| **Door 1** | Things we used to do and still can | Business as usual |
| **Door 2** | Things we couldn't do before and still can't | Business as usual |
| **Door 3** | Things we used to do but can't now | Let it go |
| **Door 4** | Things we couldn't do before but can now | Go for it |

Making visible what *won't* change helps people feel safe enough to accept what *will*.

### Authorship vs. Ownership

Simply telling people to "own the change" does not create ownership. **Authorship** — giving people genuine power to design how the change will work — creates real buy-in.

> If you say: "Here's why we have to change. You tell me how." You give them authorship. Suddenly they're not responding to change but taking control of change. *(Jason Clarke)*

### Real vs. Fake Change

- **Real change** is cultural and offered (invited)
- **Fake change** is structural and foisted (imposed)
- Structural change (reorganising charts, renaming roles) without cultural change produces no actual change

### Cultural Differences in Risk and Resistance

Research by Sulav Saha (LSE) found significant differences in how cultures respond to risk and uncertainty:
- South-Asian participants were 27% more conservative under ambiguity
- South-East-Asian participants took 24% more risk under the same conditions
- As migrants spend more time in the UK, risk tolerance decreases — suggesting environmental and social context shapes risk responses
- Contextual concerns (family, reputation, career) feature more prominently in Asian decision-making; logical metrics feature more in White-British responses

## 5.6 John Kotter on Change Leadership

### Change Management vs. Change Leadership

| | Change Management | Change Leadership |
|---|---|---|
| **Focus** | Processes, tools, mechanisms | Vision, mobilisation, momentum |
| **Scale** | Smaller changes | Larger-scale transformation |
| **Approach** | Planned and deliberate | Urgent, empowering, action-driven |
| **Driver** | Keeping projects on budget and controlled | Making change go faster and smarter |
| **Who does it** | Change management groups, consultants | Leaders with a clear vision for the future |

> Competitive pressure requiring faster, bolder responses is making change management by itself inadequate. Change leadership is the necessary solution. *(Kotter)*

### Kotter's 8-Step Model for Leading Change

1. **Create a sense of urgency** — develop open, honest dialogue about threats and opportunities; let urgency build organically through conversation
2. **Establish a guiding coalition** — identify true leaders across the organisation (not just the hierarchy); build a diverse team of influential people and ask for emotional commitment
3. **Develop a vision and strategy** — link ideas to an overall vision that people can grasp and remember; be able to describe it in five minutes or less
4. **Communicate the shared vision** — embed the vision in everything; walk the talk; use the vision daily to make decisions
5. **Empower employees** — remove structural and human barriers; recognise and reward people who make change happen
6. **Generate short-term wins** — create achievable early targets; celebrate and reward progress; use wins to build momentum
7. **Consolidate gains** — don't declare victory too early; build on each success; use 'kaizen' (continuous improvement) thinking; bring in new change agents to keep momentum fresh
8. **Anchor new approaches** — embed the change in organisational culture; include change values in hiring and training; plan for leadership succession so change legacy is not lost

> Success depends on your capacity to work with people, building relationships and commitment, and being open to the needs of those who are involved and affected. *(Kotter)*

## 5.7 Immunity to Change

### The Core Concept

Based on the work of Harvard professor Robert Kegan (*Immunity to Change*, with Lisa Lahey). The model addresses the gap between **intention** and **action** in personal and organisational change.

> If 14 frogs sat on a log and three decided to jump in the river, how many would be left? The answer is 14 — because there's a big difference between deciding to do something and actually doing it. *(Suzi Finkelstein)*

Research by Ronald Heifetz found that of heart patients told they would die without lifestyle change, only **1 in 7** made the necessary changes — and many reverted. This illustrates that the problem is not usually knowledge or willpower, but a hidden psychological immune system protecting us from anxiety.

### The Three Elements Required for Change to Stick

1. **Gut (fire in the belly)** — appetite for change must be felt deeply, not just intellectually; it must connect to people who matter
2. **Head and heart** — both reason and feeling must work together; neither alone is sufficient
3. **Hand** — intentional behavioural action; choosing actions that contradict hidden assumptions in order to test them

### The Immunity to Change Map (5 Columns)

| Column | Title | Description |
|--------|-------|-------------|
| 1 | **Improvement Goal** | The change you want to achieve — adaptive, behavioural, important, stated in the positive, and about you (not others) |
| 2 | **Doing/Not Doing Instead** | Behaviours (conscious or unconscious) that sabotage your goal — be fearlessly honest and specific |
| 3 | **Hidden Competing Commitments** | The fears and worries that arise when you imagine doing the *opposite* of column 2; the "heart of the matter"; what you are actually committed to protecting |
| 4 | **Big Assumptions** | The unconscious beliefs that make your competing commitments feel necessary — usually felt as absolutely true |
| 5 | **Strategies for Testing Assumptions** | Small, safe, modest, actionable experiments (SMART) to test whether your big assumptions are actually true |

### Three Mindset Blocks That Prevent Change (Crowell)

1. **"I can't do it"** — a fixed mindset belief that talent or genetics determines success. Overcome by adopting a **growth mindset**: effort over time produces accomplishment; rookie mistakes are learning, not proof of inadequacy.
2. **"People like me don't do things like this"** — identity threat. Overcome by finding people with a similar identity who successfully do the thing, and learning how they reconcile it.
3. **"I don't actually want to do it"** — extrinsic motivation is not enough. Overcome by building **intrinsic interest** (curiosity, genuine connection to long-term hopes) rather than relying on external validation.

## 5.8 Exploring Your Own Immunity to Change

### Guidance for Completing the Immunity Map

**Column 1 — Improvement Goal:**
- Must be adaptive (not technical) — involving personal growth
- Must matter deeply to you (8–10 out of 10)
- Must be about your behaviour, not others' behaviour
- Must be framed positively (what you are *committed to*, not what you want to *stop*)
- Example reframe: "I want to stop doubting myself" → "I am committed to backing myself and my abilities"

**Column 2 — Behaviours:**
- Be as concrete as possible — actual behaviours, not vague descriptions
- Include both what you *do* and what you *don't* do
- Do not explain or justify — just list them honestly
- Example: rather than "I get distracted" → "I browse the internet; I avoid the important task by doing trivial ones"

**Column 3 — Hidden Competing Commitments:**
- Ask: "If I imagine doing the *opposite* of what I listed in column 2, what is the most uncomfortable, worrying, or scary feeling that comes up?"
- Look for the "oh shit" feeling — where you feel completely unprotected
- If you arrive at boredom or impatience, you haven't gone deep enough
- Name the worry, then identify the competing commitment (what you are trying to *protect*)

**Column 4 — Big Assumptions:**
- These are unconscious beliefs treated as absolute truth
- Frame as: "I assume that if [competing commitment situation], then something really bad will happen"
- Challenge whether they are *always* true

**Column 5 — Strategies for Testing Assumptions:**
Use **SMART** experiments:
- **S**afe
- **M**odest
- **A**ctionable
- **R**esearch-focused (adopt a researcher's stance)
- **T**est — small experiments that provide evidence about whether the assumption is actually true

## Key Research Summaries

### Women Are Better Leaders During a Crisis
*Zenger & Folkman, HBR, December 2020*

- Data drawn from 360-degree assessments of 454 men and 366 women during March–June 2020 (first wave of COVID-19)
- Women rated significantly more effective overall (statistically significant, t=2.926, p=0.004)
- Women outscored men on 13 of 19 leadership competencies; the gap was *larger* during the pandemic than pre-pandemic
- Competencies with statistically significant differences included: takes initiative, learning agility, inspires and motivates, develops others, builds relationships, displays integrity and honesty, communicates powerfully, collaboration and teamwork, champions change, makes decisions, drives for results, values diversity, and establishes stretch goals
- Employees of female leaders had significantly higher engagement scores (55.2) compared to male leaders (49.2); overall average was 51.0
- During crisis, direct reports placed greater importance on interpersonal skills — areas where women were rated higher
- Female leaders expressed more awareness of followers' fears, concern for wellbeing, and confidence in their plans

### The Glass Cliff and Women's Precarious Leadership Appointments
*Vongas & Al Hajj, Frontiers in Psychology, 2015*

- **Glass ceiling** — invisible barrier preventing women from reaching the top
- **Glass cliff** — phenomenon where women are promoted to executive roles in *declining* organisations, placing them in positions where the risk of failure is high
- Original research by Ryan & Haslam (2005) found women were more likely to be appointed when stock market conditions were depressed in the preceding months
- The "think crisis — think female" heuristic: people associate women with crisis management, likely due to perceptions of women as more empathic
- **Evolutionary argument:** Women evolved greater empathy through parental investment and caregiving roles; humans evolved to seek women for emotional support in distress
- **Key proposition:** Women will be preferentially selected to lead *intraorganisational* crises (internal conflict, trust breakdown); men will be preferred for *interorganisational* crises (external threats, competitive warfare) — when crisis intensity is low to moderate
- At extreme crisis intensity (existential threat to the firm), stereotypically masculine traits (dominance, assertiveness) become more desirable
- Glass cliffs place women in high-failure-risk roles, perpetuating stereotypes about women's leadership ability rather than revealing the true structural conditions they inherited
