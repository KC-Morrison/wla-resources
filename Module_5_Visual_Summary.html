5.1
Creativity, Innovation & Constraints
Two distinct concepts with profound implications for how we lead, collaborate, and respond across cultures.
💡

Creativity

The conceptualisation of something entirely new from its inception. The ideas are wholly original — not building on prior work.

⚙️

Innovation

Taking established ideas and doing something new with them — a new process, new enactment, or new reconceptualisation. Less daunting than pure creativity.

🌐

Culture & Creativity

  • East: usefulness, social contribution, incremental change, tradition reinterpreted
  • West: novelty, originality, radical ideas, personal success
  • Neither is superior — both are valid and valuable

Brainstorming Challenges in Cross-Cultural Teams

Research across 55,000 professionals in 98 countries identified key barriers. Diverse teams with low Cultural Intelligence (CQ) produce less innovative ideas than homogeneous teams.

🥇

The First Idea Wins

The most assertive, often most powerful person dominates. Conformity crowds out diverse perspectives before they can be heard.

🗣️

Language Fluency

Non-native speakers must translate in and out of their language — by the time they're ready, the conversation has moved on.

🙏

Saving Face

Collectivist cultures (Asia, Latin America) prioritise harmony and conformity. Being asked to voice divergent ideas is counterintuitive.

🔭

Big Picture vs. Detail

North Americans prefer blue-sky thinking; many Germans favour detailed analysis. Neither approach is wrong — they're just different.

Three Strategies for Inclusive Brainstorming

1

Clearly Define the Objective

Ask each participant to write down their understanding of the goal beforehand. Don't move on until everyone is genuinely aligned — cultural differences often begin with clashing expectations.

2

Give Advance Warning

Don't spring sessions on the team. Allow preparation time. Offer alternative contribution formats (written lists, pre-session discussion). Brief dominant voices to hold back.

3

Insist on 100% Participation

Make the expectation explicit. The goal is input from everyone, not equal talking time. Offer collective and written formats for those from more collectivist backgrounds.

Barriers to Creativity (5.2)

🏛️

External Barriers

  • Organisational culture and norms
  • Time and resource constraints
  • Lack of shared buy-in
  • Change fatigue in the organisation
🧠

Internal Barriers

  • Fixed mindset about creative capacity
  • Lack of intrinsic motivation
  • Conflict aversion
  • Beliefs about one's role and power
5.3
When, Where & How to Get Started
Innovation requires both the right moment and a deliberate process. Radical change often backfires — incremental change, well-timed and widely owned, is far more likely to succeed.
⏰

The Right Time

Avoid periods of change fatigue. You can have the best idea and still fail if the organisation lacks the capacity to receive it. Read the moment before acting.

📍

The Right Place

Innovation isn't only for the powerful. Build influence, mobilise others, and create shared voice. Incremental adoption beats radical imposition — every time.

🤝

Shared Ownership

Staff who don't own an idea won't sustain it. Consult both influential and non-influential stakeholders. Shared ownership is the engine of lasting innovation.

Process for Getting Started (Dabrowski)

1

Take a Helicopter/Balcony View

Step back and look holistically at the organisation — its culture, current changes, its people — before proposing anything.

2

Create an Opportunity List

Identify areas that could be enhanced or improved. Broad and speculative is fine at this stage — get the ideas out.

3

Refine the List

Decide which components to move forward with and which to set aside. Consider feasibility, timing, and the culture you're working within.

4

Anticipate Challenges

Whose perspectives might not align? What mindsets will need to shift? Build a picture of the landscape before you begin advocating.

5

Build Shared Ownership — Incrementally

Consult widely. Involve those with and without formal power. Move the idea forward step by step, not in one dramatic push.

5.5
Dealing with Resistance to Change
Resistance is not irrational. Understanding the real reasons people resist — and giving them authorship over change — transforms opponents into co-designers.

Why Resistance Occurs

😟

Sense of Loss

Loss of familiar culture, role identity, or established ways of working. Change can feel like grief.

🚫

Lack of Willingness

Long-established habits are hard to shift. People may not see the need, or may not believe the change is genuine.

🔇

Lack of Transparency

When the 'why' is unclear, people fill the gap with fear. Invite dialogue — "Why are you feeling this way?" goes a long way.

📚

Lack of Skill

People may feel unable to keep up with what change demands of them. Leaders must build support mechanisms, not just expectations.

Real change sounds very much like fake change. It's hard to pick the difference. The thing is — people aren't saying they don't want change. They're saying: I want change that's real. I want something I can believe in.

— Jason Clarke, TEDxPerth

The Four Doors of Change

Making visible what won't change helps people feel safe enough to accept what will.

Door 1

Things we used to do and still can

Core practices, values, and strengths that survive the change.

Business as usual
Door 2

Things we couldn't do before and still can't

Consistent limits — ethical, practical, structural — that remain unchanged.

Business as usual
Door 3

Things we used to do but can't now

What must be released — the things the change asks people to let go of.

Let it go
Door 4

Things we couldn't do before but can now

The opportunity that change creates — new possibilities, new freedoms.

Go for it

5.6
Kotter's 8-Step Model for Leading Change
Change management controls a process. Change leadership drives a movement. In a world demanding faster, bolder responses, leadership is the necessary solution.
Change Management Change Leadership
Focus Processes, tools, staying on budget Vision, mobilisation, momentum
Scale Smaller changes Large-scale transformation
Approach Planned and deliberate Urgent and empowering
Who Change management teams Leaders with a vision

The 8 Steps

1

Create a Sense of Urgency

Open, honest dialogue about threats and opportunities. Let urgency build through conversation, not just data. Identify potential futures — good and bad.

2

Establish a Guiding Coalition

Identify true leaders across the organisation — not just by title, but by influence, expertise, and credibility. Ask for emotional commitment. Build the team before building the plan.

3

Develop a Vision and Strategy

Link ideas to a vision people can grasp and remember. The coalition must be able to describe it in five minutes or less. Determine the values central to the change.

4

Communicate the Shared Vision

Embed the vision in everything — not just in special meetings. Use it daily to make decisions. Walk the talk: what you do is more believable than what you say.

5

Empower Employees

Remove structural and human barriers. Align job descriptions, performance systems, and structures with the vision. Recognise and reward people who make change happen.

6

Generate Short-Term Wins

Create visible, achievable early targets. Each win builds momentum and counters critics. Reward the people who help deliver them — don't take wins for granted.

7

Consolidate Gains

Many change projects fail because victory is declared too early. Real change runs deep. Analyse what went right, set new goals, embrace kaizen (continuous improvement), and bring in fresh change agents.

8

Anchor New Approaches

Embed change in organisational culture — the values behind the vision must show in day-to-day work. Include change ideals in hiring and training. Plan for leadership succession so the legacy is not lost.

Success depends on your capacity to work with people, building relationships and commitment, and being open to the needs of those who are involved and affected.

— John Kotter
5.7
Immunity to Change
Based on the work of Harvard professor Robert Kegan. Your struggle to reach your goal is not a failure of willpower — it's your emotional immune system protecting you from anxiety.
1/7
Heart patients who made required lifestyle changes after being told they could die without doing so (Heifetz)
3
Elements required for change to stick: gut (fire in the belly), head & heart, and hand (intentional action)
5
Columns in the Immunity to Change map — from improvement goal to testing your biggest assumptions

The Immunity to Change Map

1
🎯

Improvement Goal

Adaptive, behavioural, important to you, stated positively, about your change — not others'. "I am committed to..."

2
⚡

Doing / Not Doing Instead

Concrete behaviours that sabotage your goal — both what you do and don't do. Fearlessly honest. No explanations.

3
❤️

Hidden Competing Commitments

The "worry box": what fears arise when you imagine doing the opposite? The heart of the matter. What are you protecting?

4
🪝

Big Assumptions

Unconscious beliefs treated as absolute truth. "I assume that if X, then something really bad will happen." Write them all.

5
🔬

Testing Assumptions

SMART: Safe, Modest, Actionable, Research-focused, Test. Small experiments to find out if your assumptions are actually true.

Three Mindset Blocks That Prevent Change (Crowell)

🧱

Block 1: "I can't do it"

A fixed mindset belief that talent or genetics determines success. Rookie mistakes become proof of inadequacy.

Overcome by: Growth mindset — effort over time creates accomplishment; mistakes are signposts, not verdicts.

🪞

Block 2: "People like me don't do this"

Identity threat — the behaviour conflicts with a hard-won sense of self. Your brain quietly steers you away.

Overcome by: Find people with a similar identity who successfully do the thing. See how they reconcile it.

💭

Block 3: "I don't actually want to"

Extrinsic motivation ("people will admire me") isn't enough when the moment of resistance arrives.

Overcome by: Build intrinsic interest — curiosity, genuine connection to your long-term hopes. Draw the bright line.

Your struggle to reach your goal is not a failure of willpower — it's your emotional immune system protecting you and warding off anxiety. If you don't discover your hidden commitments and find a new way to meet them, the immune system will.

— Suzi Finkelstein, on Kegan & Lahey's Immunity to Change

The Glass Cliff: Women's Precarious Leadership Appointments

Vongas & Al Hajj, Frontiers in Psychology, 2015

🪟

Glass Ceiling

The invisible barrier that prevents women from advancing to the highest levels of an organisation — the barrier to getting there.

🪨

Glass Cliff

When women do reach senior positions, they are disproportionately appointed to lead failing or crisis-ridden organisations — where the risk of failure is already high.

🧬

"Think Crisis — Think Female"

People associate women with crisis management, linked to perceptions of women as more empathic. This heuristic operates largely unconsciously.
