# Workshop 2: Driving Performance

> [!abstract] What this workshop covers
> Your role as a leader in driving performance, the performance management toolbelt, what actually motivates people (Herzberg, Pink and Self-Determination Theory), matching your leadership style to skill and will, giving behaviour-based feedback with SBI, analysing and addressing underperformance, and coaching with the GROW model. It closes with the GAPS development framework and cross-cultural communication.

**Central idea:** "It's the job of a manager not to light the fire of motivation, but to create an environment to let each person's own motivation flourish."

---

## W2.1 Your Role in Driving Performance

> [!note] Key insight
> Most of performance management is not formal reviews or discipline. It is the everyday, one-to-one relationship between a manager and their people. Like sailing, a well-set-up boat barely needs the rudder: small, subtle adjustments steer performance more than big corrections.

Driving performance is a core leadership responsibility, not a once-a-year event. The workshop frames the leader's role around a few reflection points:

- What is your role in driving performance?
- What common challenges do you face when driving performance?
- What are the benefits of good performance management, to the individual, the team and the organisation?

Two standing rules apply before you act on any performance concern:

> [!warning] Always
> - Check the policy
> - Check with your manager

**The employee life cycle** frames performance management across the whole relationship, from attraction and recruitment, through induction, daily management and development, to exit. Performance is influenced at every stage, not only when a problem appears.

## W2.2 The Performance Management Toolbelt

> [!note] Key insight
> Performance management is not a single conversation. It is a "toolbelt" of many components, some of which act before a person is even hired. Defining terms matters: "As Aristotle said, the beginning of wisdom is the defining of terms." (Kirk Fisher)

The toolbelt runs clockwise from before hire to formal processes. The largest tool by far is regular meetings and daily management, because that is where most performance is actually shaped.

| Tool | When / what it does |
|---|---|
| **Position description** | Before hire. Does it adequately describe the role and expectations? |
| **Policy** | Before hire. Defines the behaviours, values and standards expected. |
| **Recruitment** | How you brand the organisation to attract the right people. Recruitment is now a two-way street. |
| **Induction and training** | The first weeks are a ripe time to set expectations. Not a 500-page document dump; use mentors and regular check-ins. |
| **Regular meetings and daily management** | The largest tool. The everyday one-to-one relationship where subtle adjustments matter most. |
| **Performance development** | Formal and informal reviews. Checking in on goals and professional development. |
| **EAP (Employee Assistance Program)** | Anonymous counselling and support when someone is struggling in some area of life. Make it clearly available. |
| **Counselling and discipline** | An HR term (not psychological counselling) for managing poor performance. Accessed by the manager: it is their right and responsibility. |
| **Grievance** | Accessed by the employee. Distinct from counselling and discipline. |

> [!tip] Counselling/discipline vs grievance
> Counselling and discipline is accessed by the **manager** to address a performance problem. Grievance is accessed by the **employee**. Knowing the difference is liberating: if an employee raises a grievance mid-conversation, you can acknowledge the grievance policy exists while continuing the counselling and discipline process you are entitled to run.

## W2.3 Motivation

> [!note] Key insight
> Money matters only up to a point. Pay people enough to take the issue of money off the table, then autonomy, mastery and purpose drive real performance and satisfaction. For anything beyond mechanical tasks, bigger rewards can actually produce worse performance.

### Herzberg's Two-Factor (Hygiene-Motivation) Theory

Frederick Herzberg argued that two different factors influence people at work.

| Hygiene factors (extrinsic) | True motivators (intrinsic) |
|---|---|
| Salary, title, corner office, perks, standard remuneration | Purpose, great relationships, autonomy, creativity, developing new ideas |
| Reduce **dissatisfaction** but do not increase satisfaction | Actually drive **satisfaction** and engagement |
| Static: must keep rising to keep motivating (salary up and up and up) | Deeper and more durable |

> [!example] Anthony's story
> A new HR graduate hated a hot, dusty, repetitive factory job and quit. The employer offered a raise, bonus and company car; he stayed, then quit three months later anyway. Hygiene factors were not enough to keep him.

### Daniel Pink and the RSA "Drive" evidence

Studies (MIT students, then replicated in rural Madurai, India, and funded by the Federal Reserve Bank) found:

| Task type | Effect of bigger rewards |
|---|---|
| Simple, mechanical, "if-then" / algorithmic | Higher pay, better performance (works as expected) |
| Anything requiring even rudimentary cognitive or creative thinking | Larger reward led to **poorer** performance |

The three factors science shows lead to better performance and satisfaction:

- **Autonomy** the desire to be self-directed. Management gets compliance; self-direction gets engagement. (Example: Atlassian's quarterly 24-hour "work on anything you want" day produced fixes and new product ideas that would never otherwise emerge.)
- **Mastery** the urge to get better at something. Engaged by having a sense of progress and metrics; this is why people play instruments on weekends.
- **Purpose** something bigger than yourself, often tied to relationships and to your values.

> [!tip] Applying autonomy, mastery and purpose
> You don't need all three at once. A common team meeting can use all of them: invite ideas (autonomy), let people pick tasks they are good at (mastery), and hold each other to account next week (purpose/relationship). Part of a leader's job is helping people build intrinsic motivation for the things they simply have to do.

### Self-Determination Theory (Ryan & Deci)

Self-Determination Theory identifies three innate psychological needs that, when satisfied, yield enhanced self-motivation and mental health, and when thwarted, diminish them:

- **Competence**
- **Autonomy**
- **Relatedness**

Social contexts either facilitate or forestall the natural processes of self-motivation and healthy psychological development.

### Discretionary performance

People will work **hard for a pay cheque, harder for a person, and hardest for a purpose or cause**. The gap between the minimum and the effort people choose to give is discretionary performance, and leaders unlock it through the true motivators.

> [!example] Case studies: Catherine and Joe
> **Catherine** (early 20s, new post-graduate, eager, first in and last out) and **Joe** (late 40s, eight years' service, avoids change, family-oriented, leaves at 5 on the dot). The exercise: what are the potential motivators for each, and what strategies would work best to motivate each? The point is that motivation is individual.

## W2.4 The Skill-Will Model

> [!note] Key insight
> There is no single best leadership style. Match your style to the person and the task, based on their **skill** (can they do it?) and their **will** (do they want to?).

Based on Max Landsberg's *The Tao of Coaching*.

```
                 HIGH WILL
                     |
       GUIDE         |      DELEGATE
   (low skill,       |   (high skill,
    high will)       |    high will)
 ------------------- + -------------------  
       DIRECT        |      EXCITE
   (low skill,       |   (high skill,
    low will)        |    low will)
                     |
                 LOW WILL
   LOW SKILL  <----------------->  HIGH SKILL
```

| Quadrant | Skill / Will | Leadership focus |
|---|---|---|
| **Guide** | Low skill, high will | Invest time, train and coach; nurture the willing learner and build capability. |
| **Delegate** | High skill, high will | Empower and trust; low involvement, give autonomy. |
| **Direct** | Low skill, low will | Provide clear instruction and close structure and supervision. |
| **Excite** | High skill, low will | Find the reason for low will; re-motivate, rebuild confidence and engagement. |

**Skill-Will Style Inventory:** for each team-member/activity combination, choose the most appropriate style and note when and how you will next apply it.

## W2.5 Providing Feedback (SBI) and Managing Underperformance

> [!note] Key insight
> The most effective, constructive feedback is behaviour-based: it tells the person what they actually said or did, not your interpretation or judgement of it. SBI works for both recognition and correction.

### The SBI model

**Situation → Behaviour → Impact**

| Element | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Situation** | Anchor in time and place. |
| **Behaviour** | Observable action. "Think of playing back a video recorder using only words." |
| **Impact** | What I felt or thought, and my perception of the impact on others, the task and the work environment. |

After delivering SBI, explore the person's perspective and encourage them to consider what they might do as a result.

> [!example] SBI in practice
> "At the end of the team meeting this morning (S), you gave a concise summary of the key action steps (B). I was really glad you did as it answered my outstanding questions, and it seemed to bring a clear sense of closure for the others (I)."

### Analysis of underperformance

A structured process to check that you and the organisation have done everything required for a person to be able to perform, before deciding the most appropriate course of action.

> [!warning] Before giving significant or difficult feedback, every time
> 1. Check with your manager
> 2. Check your policies
> 3. Check the award
> 4. Check recent legislation and rulings; get HR advice when possible

**Document carefully.** Relevant records include verbal statements or gestures, performance metrics, client/customer feedback, resource costs and usage, labour hours and wages, emails, training certificates, and team and individual performance plans.

## W2.6 Leader as Coach: The GROW Model

> [!note] Key insight
> Coaching is about empowering others to own the problem and create their own solutions, not solving it for them. "Investing 10 minutes of coaching can actually save an hour." (Landsberg)

GROW is a model for a coaching conversation. The first half is exploratory and divergent; the second half converges to a meaningful, actionable outcome. It is fluid, not strictly linear: you can move back and forth as understanding shifts.

| Stage | Purpose | Sample questions |
|---|---|---|
| **Goal** | Set goals, write them down, establish what the person wants from the session. | "What would you like to achieve?" "What would need to happen for this time to feel well spent?" |
| **Reality** | Let them tell their story; invite self-assessment; discuss effects. | "Where are you in relation to your goal?" "What have you tried so far?" "What is working well?" |
| **Options** | Brainstorm options, ask (don't tell), empower and ensure choice. | "What are your options?" "What else might work?" "Would you like suggestions from me?" |
| **Wrap-up** | Identify specific steps and obstacles; write an action plan. | "What is one small step you'll take now?" "On a scale of 1 to 10, how committed are you?" |

> [!tip] Peer coaching tips and common mistakes (Suzi Finkelstein)
> - **Spend enough time on the goal.** Put your foot on the brake; understand why this goal, why now.
> - **Don't get seduced into expertise.** Resist "what would you do?" Redirect; empower them to own it.
> - **Watch for loaded or leading questions** ("have you tried...", "would you consider..."). Stay curious and open.
> - **Watch for loaded silences.** The pause gives the brain space to calibrate. Go slow; don't fill it.
> - **Stay neutral; don't collude.** Empathy builds rapport, but don't take on their story as your own.
> - **Use GROW flexibly.** Move backwards and forwards as the real goal emerges.
> - **Give yourself a break.** It's a learning curve; gather feedback; find your own style.

## W2.7 Additional Resources: GAPS and Development Priorities

> [!note] Key insight
> Development happens in the creative tension between where you are and where you (and others) want you to be. GAPS maps four views to surface that tension.

### The GAPS framework (Lisa Geerlings)

A two-by-two matrix comparing **now vs future** and **my view vs others' views**.

```
                NOW              FUTURE
           +----------------+----------------+
PERSON'S   |   ABILITIES    |     GOALS      |
  VIEW     | my view of my  | my goals,      |
           | strengths &    | values,        |
           | weaknesses     | aspirations    |
           +----------------+----------------+
OTHER'S    |  PERCEPTIONS   | SUCCESS FACTORS|
  VIEW     | how others see | what the org / |
           | my strengths & | role requires  |
           | weaknesses     | for success    |
           +----------------+----------------+
```

- **G - Goals:** the future as I see it. What I want to achieve.
- **A - Abilities:** now, as I see it. My strengths and weaknesses.
- **P - Perceptions:** now, as others see it. How others rate my strengths and gaps.
- **S - Success factors:** the future as others (the organisation/manager) define it for a role.

Look for the **creative tension** between these four areas: between goals and abilities, between self-view and others' perceptions, and between personal goals and organisational success factors. If you feel stuck, increase the tension by seeking more feedback or setting more aspirational goals.

### Prioritising development goals (Paul Larkin)

When several development needs emerge, prioritise on two scales: **payback** (return to the organisation and person) and **ease of learning**.

```
            HIGH PAYBACK
                 |
   Worthwhile    |   ** FOCUS HERE **
   (harder slog, |   (high payback,
    high payback)|    easy to learn)
 --------------- + ---------------  
   Avoid         |   Low priority
   (low payback, |   (easy but
    hard)        |    low payback)
                 |
            LOW PAYBACK
   HARD  <------------------>  EASY
```

Start top-right: high payback and relatively easy to learn gives the best return on investment and delivers early success, which builds motivation and confidence. If nothing sits there, stay in the high-payback column even if it is a harder slog. Don't waste effort on low-payback skills, whether easy or hard.

## W2.8 Cross-Cultural Communication

> [!note] Key insight
> A difficult conversation is harder by an order of magnitude across cultures: you must handle a thorny subject while navigating cultural trip wires. In coaching, "culture is irrelevant" in the sense that coaching happens at the level of the individual; use cultural knowledge to customise, not to stereotype.

### Four common communication trip wires (Hahn & Molinsky, HBR)

| Trip wire | One pole | Other pole |
|---|---|---|
| **Business vs relationship** | Get down to business quickly (e.g. US) | Build the relationship first (e.g. Mexico) |
| **Direct vs indirect** | State the problem plainly | Use hints and general statements to protect face (e.g. Japan) |
| **Low vs high context** | Meaning is in the exact words (e.g. Canada) | Meaning is read across many frequencies and settings (e.g. South Korea) |
| **Informality vs formality** | Casual approach valued | Formality signals respect |

The danger in each: a direct, task-oriented person may seem insensitive; an indirect, relationship-oriented person may seem scattered or evasive.

### Executive coaching across cultures (David B. Peterson)

Use cross-cultural knowledge to **customise** coaching to each individual rather than to make assumptions. Peterson's provocation that "culture is irrelevant in coaching" makes the point that culture is a group-level phenomenon while coaching occurs at the level of the individual: the coach's challenge is to get to know the actual person in front of them.
