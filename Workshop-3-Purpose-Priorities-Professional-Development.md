# Purpose, Priorities and Professional Development

> **The question this guide is built around:** *What am I committing to do in order to be the leader I want to be?*

A standalone guide to the frameworks, models and exercises for identifying fulfilment in your work, managing your priorities, and building a professional development plan you will actually follow. You do not need any prior material to use it.

---

## What This Covers

Developing the skills to hone a competitive edge: how to develop and refine your professional goals, build your professional capabilities, and establish the critical behaviours for setting personal work objectives and prioritising workload for optimum achievement.

By the end of it you should be able to:

- Develop capacity to identify and pursue fulfilment in your current role
- Demonstrate capacity to conduct productive discussions
- Apply methods for effectively managing your workload and your schedule
- Formulate and commit to a professional development plan, and design professional development tasks

Three questions to hold throughout:

- Where am I now?
- Where do I want to be?
- What do I need to focus on in order to achieve it?

## 1. The Third Space

*Model adapted from Fraser (2012).*

The Third Space is the transition between the First Space (what you have just come from) and the Second Space (what you are moving into). Most of us move between the two without pausing, carrying the residue of the last meeting, task or conversation straight into the next one. The Third Space makes that transition deliberate, and it has three moves.

| Move | Prompts |
|---|---|
| **Reflect** | What did I learn? What actions do I need to follow up? |
| **Rest** | How can I make myself still and present? What's needed? |
| **Reset** | How will I show up? What's my intention? |

**Exercise.** Before any significant block of work, complete this sentence:

> *I need to pay attention to ..................... in order to gain the most from this.*

## 2. Finding the Sweet Spot

*Model: Dave Pollard, "Finding the Sweet Spot" (Chelsea Green Publishing, 2008).*

People often find themselves in a role that doesn't fulfil their expectations. Sometimes it's because they are doing what they are good at but not what they are passionate about. Sometimes it's because they are working passionately with their gifts and talents but are frustrated, as there is no clear avenue for the work, or no clear sense that the work is really needed.

Your sweet spot is where three things overlap.

### Two ways in

Before working the model, two useful framings:

- **The Crossroads of Should and Must** (Elle Luna). At most decision points there is the thing you feel you *should* do and the thing you *must* do. The book's subtitle is the instruction: find and follow your passion.
- **A Job / A Career / A Calling** (attributed to Stefan Sagmeister). The same work can be held three different ways. Which one you are in is not fixed by the role. It is partly a choice about the meaning you make of it.

> "The only way to do great work is to love what you do."
> - Steve Jobs

### The three circles

**GIFTS - What do you do uniquely well?**

The greatest part of the challenge of finding the sweet spot is our ignorance of what our gifts, our natural talents, the things we do easily, actually are. We live in a world that is terribly specialised, and we are driven early in life to pick one of a few narrow career channels suited to the needs of large, impersonal, industrial employers, and to stop dreaming about doing anything else.

Ask yourself:

- What have people said you are especially good at?
- What activities have you received the most praise for, in work or outside?
- What tasks come easiest to you, whether you enjoy them or not?
- Intuitively, if someone asked you what your "natural talent" was, how would you answer?
- What hobbies or recreational activities do you think, or have others suggested, you might be good enough to make a living at? Why?
- What are you most capable working with: people, tools, ideas or information?

**PASSION - What do you love doing?**

Although our intellect, which drives our gifts, evolves rather slowly over our lives, our emotions, which underlie our passions, can change quickly, as our bodies change and our experiences colour what we care about.

Ask yourself:

- In other people's observation, what do you seem to enjoy the most? What activities cause you to smile or sing while you do them?
- What tasks do you take on with most relish, whether you are good at them or not? What do people get you to do because they know you will take it on enthusiastically?
- Intuitively, if someone asked you what your "ideal job" might be, how would you answer?
- What hobbies or recreational activities do you, or did you, most enjoy? Why?
- What do you most enjoy working with: people, tools, ideas or information?
- If you suddenly received a lot of money, or a lot of power, what would you do with it, and what would you start doing that you're not doing now, and why?

A useful marker for passion: the things you start intending to do for half an hour, and three hours later you have lost track of time entirely.

**PURPOSE - What's needed that you care about?**

Your purpose must be discovered, not invented. It starts with needs, not with solutions. Your purpose gives focus and meaning to your life and directs your decisions on what to do.

Ask yourself:

- What stirs you? What's going on in the world that makes you want to get involved?
- What "aha!" moments or callings have come to you in moments of reflection or crisis?
- What, most of all, do you think is needed in the world, in your country, your community, your profession, your current workplace?
- What do you feel responsible for, that you think you might be able to help improve?

Purpose is the list most people struggle with. It is often easier to start from what you don't like to see in the world, and what you think the world could be better at.

### Reading the diagram

Draw three overlapping circles, one for each. Every position on the diagram has a distinct emotional signature, and recognising the feeling is often how you work out where you actually are.

| Position | What you're using | How it feels |
|---|---|---|
| Gifts only | Gifts | **Frustration** - you keep getting pulled back to the things you're good at without getting to tap into your purpose or passion |
| Passion only | Passion | **Self-indulgent** - good for you for short periods, but little fulfilment in it for other people |
| Purpose only | Purpose | **Self-sacrificing** - working for a purpose no one else seems to care about, that you may not be good at or passionate about |
| Passion + Purpose | Both, no gifts | **Disappointment** |
| Passion + Gifts | Both, no purpose | **Unappreciated** - no real need in the world for it |
| Purpose + Gifts | Both, no passion | **Disengaged** |
| **All three** | Gifts + Passion + Purpose | **The Sweet Spot** |

*Worked example of the "gifts only" trap:* a client who was very good at accounting. Every time a finance job came up, even on a committee, they were asked to do it. They were good at it, but it did nothing for them. That's frustration, not fulfilment.

**Exercise.** Make your three lists, then place a dot on the diagram where you see yourself in your career right now. Most of us spend at least half our waking hours at work, and there's no sense being miserable for half your waking hours.

### The stonecutter story

A man walking through the desert comes across three workers cutting stones and asks each what they are doing.

- The first says, *"I'm earning a living."*
- The second says, *"I'm trying to be the best stone cutter the world has ever seen."*
- The third looks up to the hillside, points to the sky, and says, *"I'm building a cathedral."*

Three people, the same task, each making meaning of it differently. Sometimes a sense of purpose is not about changing what you do. It is about finding the purpose in what you are already choosing to do, and anchoring in that as a source of motivation.

### Why leaders in particular need a sense of purpose

> "Leadership oftentimes is a passionate and consuming activity... So strong are the emotions of leadership, they can overwhelm the person who has not developed a sufficiently broad sense of purpose... Preserving a sense of purpose helps one take setbacks and failures in stride... A sense of purpose provides the ongoing capacity to generate new possibilities."
> - Heifetz (1994)

Ron Heifetz's work on adaptive leadership makes the point plainly: the more into leadership you get, the more complicated the decisions become and the harder and lonelier the job becomes. It is easy to get blown off course, sucked back into management time, distracted, or pulled by other people away from what you truly think is most important.

So it's worth answering, before you need the answer:

- What is my purpose for being a leader? Why do I do what I do?
- On a bad day, when I'm in danger of getting blown off course, what's going to keep me getting out of bed in the morning?

*One person's answer, as an example of the form:* "I don't believe that happiness and work need to be mutually exclusive. I believe that those two things can go together, and I'm on a mission to help people see that that's possible."

### A formula for writing your purpose statement

*Adapted from Carolyn Tate, The Purpose Project.*

> **Purpose = verb + intention (what) + outcome (beneficiary)**

Examples:

- *"To create a brilliant connected future for everyone"*
- *"Make safety available to every worker in the world"*
- *"Enabling executives to flourish"*
- *"To write books that truly matter"*
- *"To unearth a higher purpose in people and organisations"*

### Two related ideas

- **Global orientation.** Global boundaries are dissolving, with implications for careers. It might involve developing a "global work orientation", an ability to "seek commonality between cultures", and to "embrace positive indifference". Cultural agility in global contexts can be measured, focusing on self-management, relationship management and task management.
- **Ikigai.** A comparable model to the Sweet Spot, often translated as one's reason for being.

### Take it to your team

- Where do you currently sit within the three circles? If you're having moments in the middle, what do you need to stay there? If you're not, what can you do to get there?
- Ask your team: are you engaging your gifts, your passion and your purpose in your current role? If not, what might you need to do to get there?

## 3. Putting Energy Into Your Vision

> "Logic will get you from A to B... Imagination is everything, it will take you everywhere. It is the preview of life's coming attractions."
> - Albert Einstein

> "Action without vision is only passing time, vision without action is merely dreaming, but vision with action can change the world."
> - Nelson Mandela

> "The gap between vision and current reality is also a source of energy. If there were no gap, there would be no need for any action to move towards the vision."
> - Peter Senge

### Creative tension

Creative tension is the internal mechanism that helps us achieve what we want. It requires two things:

1. **A vision** of what you want: a goal, an aspiration, something you're striving towards
2. **A clear, honest understanding of current reality**: all the strengths you have to draw on, all the weaknesses you have to overcome, and the external challenges you also need to face up to

When you have both, and there is a sufficient gap between them, that gap sets up internal tension. It is that creative tension that gives the motivation and impetus to create change and make things happen.

> **A vision without current reality is really just a dream. Current reality without a vision is just stuck, complacent, or coasting.**

*Example.* You might aspire to be fit and healthy. But if you haven't faced up to the fact that you overindulged over the holidays, gained weight and stopped following your exercise programme, that aspiration is a pipe dream. You need to honestly assess where you are now in relation to the vision if you actually want to motivate yourself to do anything differently.

### A case study in vision, obstacles and persistence

Diana Nyad swam from Cuba to Florida at 64, on her fifth attempt, having first dreamed of and attempted the swim in her twenties. No one had done it, and swimmers had been trying since 1950.

What she was up against: over 100 miles of open ocean, box jellyfish carrying the deadliest venom in the ocean (she had nearly died from them on a previous attempt), sharks, hypothermia, and the Gulf Stream, the most unpredictable current on the planet. The crossing took 53 hours. Her team was around 30 people, including shark experts and her head handler and best friend, Bonnie Stoll.

Things worth taking from it:

- **The mantra was "Find a way."** Not "win", not "finish". Find a way. Bonnie's version, at the start: *"Let's find our way to Florida."*
- **A cardinal rule: she is never told how far it is.** Because nobody knows what will happen between this point and that point.
- **The vision was specific and it was held for decades.** She was clear for a very long time about exactly what she wanted.

The three things she said when she reached the beach:

1. **Never, ever give up.** *"I didn't give up. There was action behind these words."*
2. **You can chase your dreams at any age; you're never too old.**
3. **It looks like the most solitary endeavour in the world, and in many ways it is. And in other ways, and the most important ways, it's a team.** *"We did it. I didn't do it. We did it."*

> "When you reach for the horizon, as I've proven, you may not get there. But what a tremendous build of character and spirit that you lay down; what a foundation you lay down in reaching for those horizons."

> "When you achieve your dreams, it's not so much what you get as who you have become in achieving them."
> - Henry David Thoreau

She also paraphrased Teddy Roosevelt: you can sit back in your comfortable chair and be the critic, be the observer, while the brave one gets in the ring and engages and gets bloody and gets dirty and fails over and over again, but yet isn't afraid and isn't timid and lives life in a bold way.

### On the moment of commitment

> "Until one is committed, there is hesitancy, the chance to draw back, always ineffectiveness. Concerning all acts of initiative (and creation), there is one elementary truth, the ignorance of which kills countless ideas and splendid plans: that the moment one definitely commits oneself, then providence moves too. A whole stream of events issues from the decision, raising in one's favour all manner of unforeseen incidents, meetings and material assistance, which no man could have dreamt would have come his way. I learned a deep respect for one of Goethe's couplets: Whatever you can do or dream you can, begin it. Boldness has genius, power and magic in it!"
> - W.H. Murray, *The Scottish Himalayan Expedition*

### A cultural caveat on visioning

Perceptions of long-term and short-term goals differ, for individuals and for organisations. Depending on whether someone comes from a short-term or long-term orientation culture, visions of the future may differ in reach.

Trompenaars' concept of internal and external direction matters here too:

| Orientation | Belief | Example cultures |
|---|---|---|
| **Internal direction** | People believe they can control their environment to achieve their goals | US, UK, Australia |
| **External direction** | People believe they must work *with* their environment; winning isn't as important as maintaining a strong relationship | China, Russia, Saudi Arabia |

So **how** people expect to achieve a vision may differ, even when they agree on the vision itself.

*Related model: Trompenaars and Hampden-Turner's Seven Dimensions of Culture, developed to help understand and manage cultural differences. Useful if you work globally or manage a diverse group.*

### Two exercises

- **Personal Vision.** Capture what you want for yourself. Use images, symbols, words, whatever helps you best express it.
- **Personal Leadership Story.** Write from a point in the future, looking back at what you have done and what you have accomplished.

### Take it to your team

- What's your vision? What's your goal for your career? Start articulating what you want to achieve, and build your own creative tension.
- In considering your vision, how much control do you believe you have to create change?
- Build creative tension in a team by asking three questions about a project or goal: What is the vision of what we're trying to achieve? Where are we currently at now? How can we best move from where we are now towards our vision?

## 4. Managing Priorities

### From time management to task management

The old approach to time management was to list all your tasks, rate them A, B or C in terms of importance, and plan them into your day. It was about action plans and to-do lists. While to-do lists may still be worthwhile, a better approach is to think of it not as time management but as **task management**, or more completely, as **time and energy management**.

Diagnostic questions to ask before you plan anything:

- Is this really a necessary task?
- Am I spending my time in "fire fighting" mode?
- Do I work on band-aid fixes rather than looking at the cause of problems?
- Do I feel guilty taking time out for myself?
- Do I put off some tasks that I know are important but may be perceived as time wasting, or cause people to think I'm not busy?
- Am I saying yes to doing other people's work because they have got themselves into trouble or cannot manage their own tasks?
- Am I working on certain activities because they are enjoyable rather than because they will make a difference?

### The Covey Matrix

*From Stephen Covey's "The 7 Habits of Highly Effective People". One habit of the effective leader is to "Put First Things First": work out what is important to you personally and professionally and put your efforts there, rather than being pulled away or sidetracked by other people's priorities or by fun but wasteful activities.*

Two definitions do all the work in this model:

- **Important:** activities that have outcomes that lead to the achievement of **your** goals
- **Urgent:** activities that demand immediate attention, and are often associated with the achievement of **someone else's** goals

We spend much of our time on urgent activities because these are the squeaky wheels that get the grease. They demand attention because if we don't deal with them immediately there are significant negative consequences. The matrix exists to help us overcome that natural tendency to serve the urgent at the expense of the important.

|  | **Urgent** | **Not Urgent** |
|---|---|---|
| **Important** | **Quadrant I - Demand**<br>Crisis, pressing problems, deadline projects | **Quadrant II - The Zone**<br>Planning, prevention, relationship building, new opportunities, learning and development |
| **Not Important** | **Quadrant III - Illusion**<br>Interruptions, other people's priorities, some calls and mail, pressing matters without impact, popular activities | **Quadrant IV - Escape**<br>Trivia, busy work, time wasters, private calls, internet surfing, pleasant activities |

**How to work each quadrant:**

- **Quadrant I, Urgent and Important.** These fall into two types: unforeseeable issues and crises, and activities left to the last minute. Make space for the first type; plan ahead and avoid procrastination for the second. If you spend a lot of your time here you are likely to experience burnout over time. Look at how you can delegate and plan strategically so these move into Quadrant II.
- **Quadrant II, Not Urgent and Important.** These activities help you achieve your personal and professional goals. This quadrant allows you to plan for improvement and growth. **The challenge is to create as much time as possible for this quadrant. This is being proactive**, and it is where your strategic efforts are best used.
- **Quadrant III, Urgent and Not Important.** These stop you from achieving your goals. Challenge these tasks. Can they be rescheduled, or can someone else do them?
- **Quadrant IV, Not Urgent and Not Important.** These activities are distractions and should be avoided.

**A worked example of moving a task.** A business used to spend a solid two weeks out of every quarter hand-mailing its catalogue, physically stuffing pieces of paper into envelopes. It consumed enormous time, felt urgent, and generated very little response: urgent and not important. When email arrived, the same content went out as an email with a link back, response rates rose sharply, and the activity became not urgent and important. It ended up a major feature of how they did business.

A smaller version of the same move: coming home tonight is neither urgent nor important. Coming home with flowers moves it up into important.

**The exercise.** List the main tasks you do and plot them into the four quadrants. Then work out **what percentage of your time you spend in each quadrant**, and identify strategies for moving tasks into the not urgent and important quadrant wherever possible.

### The myth of multitasking

Research in neuroscience tells us the brain doesn't do tasks simultaneously, as we thought or hoped it might. We just **switch tasks quickly**. Each time we move between activities there is a stop/start process in the brain. Rather than saving time, it costs time, even in microseconds. It is less efficient, we make more mistakes, and over time it saps our energy.

**Test it on yourself:**

1. Draw two horizontal lines. Have someone time you. On the first line write "I am a great multitasker". On the second write the numbers 1 to 20 sequentially. This usually takes about 20 seconds.
2. Draw two more lines. This time alternate: write the letter "I", then the number "1", then the letter "a", then the number "2", and so on, changing lines each time.

Your time will typically be **double or more**, with errors and frustration, because you have to rethink what comes next on every switch.

That is switch-tasking on something very simple. It is exactly what happens when you attempt to do several more complex things at once. The remedy: give yourself a time limit, say 10 or 45 minutes, focus on a single task, and see whether you don't complete it better, faster and with less energy.

### Take it to your team

- Where do you currently put most of your energy? What might you need to do in order to work more in the High Importance, Low Urgency quadrant?
- Share the matrix with your team and discuss where the team is putting its energy, and what you may need to do to work more in that quadrant.

## 5. GROW Coaching

*Model adapted from Whitmore (1992). The four stages sit around a centre marked FOCUS.*

| Stage | What happens |
|---|---|
| **G - Goal** | Set goals, write them down, and establish what the person wants from the session |
| **R - Reality** | Let them tell their story. Invite self-assessment, ask what's happening, and discuss the effects of the situation |
| **O - Options** | Brainstorm options. Ask, don't tell. Empower and ensure choice. How can you move toward your goal? What has worked in the past? |
| **W - Wrap Up** | Identify specific steps and any obstacles. Write an action plan |

### Practising it in triads

- Break into groups of 3
- Allocate roles: **Coach**, **Coachee**, **Observer**
- 15 minutes of coaching followed by 5 minutes of feedback
- Change roles and repeat twice more, so everyone holds every role

### Coaching tips

- Be clear on what outcome they are seeking from the conversation, a list of ideas, greater understanding, a plan, before you get too far into it
- Ask open, generative questions
- **Resist the urge to offer advice.** Let them come up with their own solutions
- Get them to commit to something as a next step by the end of the session. Anything, even reading or thinking about something, might be enough
- Notice what it is like for you in each role. What feels natural or awkward? What can you learn about yourself?

### The debrief structure

- **WWW** - What is Working Well?
- **EBI** - What would be **E**ven **B**etter **I**f?

## 6. Running a Leadership Project

A leadership project is an outcome-driven activity focused on an opportunity to lead positive change within your organisation, as well as on your own leadership growth. The point is not to add substantially to your workload, but to have somewhere deliberate to apply new skills and strategies to your leadership practice.

### Project criteria

- **Reflect, analyse, monitor and lead change using a strong evidence base.** Keeping a leadership diary throughout is the simplest way to build one.
- **Apply a minimum of two models or leadership frameworks** to the work, drawn from your learning or your own leadership research.
- **Deliver a tangible outcome** in an area you have identified as needing leadership development.

### Choosing a focus

- The focus can be either personal or professional
- It could be a piece of work you are already doing, or something you can start now
- You do not need to complete it inside any fixed time frame
- It can have a purely personal leadership development focus rather than being a work project

### Six questions to structure the reflection

These work equally well for a short presentation and for a written report.

1. **Context** - What is your project, why did you choose it, who was involved?
2. **Progress** - What steps have you taken towards it?
3. **Wins and challenges** - Your successes and setbacks, and how you overcame them
4. **Reflections** - What stood out to you: the challenges you overcame, how you built relationships, how your team was able to work together
5. **Next steps** - Where to from here?
6. **How did I implement my learning?** - What did you actually apply? How did facilitators, a peer coach or your colleagues assist you? What resources were most beneficial to your leadership development?

In writing it up, reflect on the project against the frameworks and resources you have been using. In particular, consider **the impact of your behaviours on the achievement of your goals**, and ways to explore and overcome your own areas of resistance.

*A useful discipline: cap the written reflection at around 1500 words. Clear, concise and focused writing is what makes it worth rereading.*

### If you present it

- Five minutes maximum
- Make it engaging: use visual aids, props, stories, metaphors or role play
- Get the audience involved and learning, rather than reporting at them

## 8. Supporting Research

Two pieces of research that give context to the themes above. The connections drawn here are indicative.

### HILDA Statistical Report 2021 (Melbourne Institute)

*Wilkins, Vera-Toscano, Botha and Dahmann (2021), Selected Findings from Waves 1 to 19. A nationally representative longitudinal study of Australian households. All data pre-dates COVID-19; the most recent wave was collected between 30 July 2019 and 9 February 2020.*

**Time stress.** Defined as reporting that you "often" or "almost always" feel rushed or pressed for time.

- Women consistently report higher time stress than men, by about **7 to 8 percentage points**. In 2019, **38% of women** were time stressed versus **29% of men** (in 2001: 39% and 34%). The overall decline since 2001 has been almost entirely among men.
- The **35 to 44 age group** has the highest rates, with almost half consistently in time stress. The 25 to 34 and 45 to 54 groups are consistently over 40%. Those aged 65 and over report the least, around 12 to 13%.
- Higher education is associated with **more** time stress: women and men with a Bachelor's degree or higher are **11.1 and 10.0 percentage points** more likely to report time stress than those who did not complete high school.
- Employment is a major driver: employed women are **21.4 percentage points** more likely to be time stressed than unemployed women (men: 16.5 points).
- Each additional hour of weekly housework increases the probability of being time stressed by **0.2 percentage points**.
- Resident children under 15 matter more for women: women with three or more are **24.5 percentage points** more likely to be time stressed (men: 14.7 points).
- Time stress is associated with worse mental health, worse self-rated health, less physical activity and lower life satisfaction.

**Unpaid work.** Relevant to any honest accounting of total load.

- In every relationship type except dependent children, women average more unpaid work than men.
- For couples with dependent children, the gap has narrowed from **28.8 hours per week in 2002 to 20.9 hours in 2019**, mostly because partnered women reduced their unpaid work (53.5 to 48.7 hours) rather than because men increased theirs (24.7 to 27.8 hours).
- In 2019, partnered women with dependent children averaged **75.7 hours** of total work per week, paid plus unpaid, versus **73.7 hours** for partnered men with dependent children.
- Where the youngest child is under 6, women averaged **60.8 hours** of unpaid work per week versus **31.6 hours** for men. The total working time gap only disappears entirely once the youngest child is 13 to 17.

**Self-control.** Relevant to commitment and follow-through.

- Self-control allows people to regulate behaviour, override impulses and achieve long-term goals.
- Average self-control is **3.5 on a 1 to 5 scale**; women 3.5, men 3.4. It increases with age, from 3.2 (15 to 24) to 3.7 (65 and over).
- A 1-point increase is associated with **more than half a point increase in life satisfaction** for both women and men, plus lower likelihood of obesity, smoking and excessive alcohol consumption, and a 7.3 percentage point higher probability of being able to raise $3,000 for an emergency from savings.

### BCG and Heidrick & Struggles: "Learning from Women Who've Made It to the Top in Tech" (24 June 2021)

*Awad, Hennessy and Kim Morse. Based on a survey of more than 750 female and male tech leaders (457 women, 300 men), in-depth interviews with more than 70 women in tech leadership, and Leadership Accelerator Questionnaire data for 147 female and 435 male technology leaders. US data.*

**Context.** Women make up almost half (47%) of the US workforce but hold less than one-third (28%) of leadership positions in tech. Firms where 30% of leaders are women have a 15% increase in profitability compared with similar firms with no female leaders (Peterson Institute for International Economics).

**Findings:**

- **There is no ambition gap.** Roughly the same percentage of women and men, 62% and 67% respectively, were trying for a promotion.
- **Early promotions matter more to women.** 44% of women said their first promotion was the most important, versus 32% of men. Men tended to point to later promotions.
- **Risk is approached differently.** 66% of men were very comfortable taking risks and managing ambiguity, versus 56% of women. Only 41% of women went for multiple promotions where they did not meet all the qualifications, versus 51% of men. Among technologists specifically the gap widens (55% of women versus 70% of men); among business unit and sales leaders it virtually disappears.
- **Women rely on people, men on self-confidence.** When pursuing a promotion without meeting all qualifications, 35% of men cited individual confidence versus 17% of women. Women drew more on colleagues and mentors (49% versus 45%), external sources (17% versus 11%) and household members (16% versus 7%).
- **Women change employers less often**, despite both groups agreeing job changes drive advancement. 47% of women had fewer than 3 employers versus 19% of men; 24% of women had more than 5 versus 43% of men.
- **Mentor relationships are shorter for women.** 14% of women had a mentor relationship lasting more than 5 years versus 29% of men.
- **Technical skills are weighted differently.** 23% of women cited advanced technical skills as most important to their success versus 13% of men. Both cited "setting a vision" as the single most important factor overall.

**What the interviewees said they learned:**

- **Hone an effective communication style.** Start with the conclusion rather than circling around it with context first. *"Start with the headline, don't think you have to build to your conclusion. Start there, then back it up. Their ears get tired, they want the punch line."* And skip the apologies.
- **Push for constructive feedback.** Seek and act on criticism from managers, colleagues, sponsors and mentors. One interviewee went back to a hiring manager to ask why she was passed over: *"Asking for that feedback was the best decision I ever made."*
- **Master the art of the humble brag.** Condition yourself to express your achievements. Some found it easier by focusing on the accomplishments of their team.
- **Embrace who you are.** *"When you're insecure, you make yourself into what you think they want. It's exhausting."*
- **Embrace the stretch role.** *"People look for the perfect job with the least risk. I take the ones that are difficult because those are the ones that will stretch me."*

One reframing worth keeping: *"Among women in the tech world, there is more often a context gap than a confidence gap."*
