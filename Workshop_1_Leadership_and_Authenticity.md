# Workshop 1: Leadership and Authenticity
*WLA Executive Ready Program*

---

## Workshop 1 Overview

In this workshop you explore what it takes to be an effective leader using a strengths-based approach. The workshop introduces a values-based leadership framework and takes a deep dive into the relationship between authenticity and effective leadership. The central question running through the day is: *what sort of leader do I want to be?*

### Learning Intentions

- Identify and consider characteristics and principles of leadership
- Identify and define what moral purpose means to you
- Differentiate between leadership and management
- Explore values from both follower and leader perspectives
- Explore the impact of espoused values versus actual behaviours
- Develop a clear, workable leadership vision

### The Day at a Glance

The workshop runs through the following segments: Welcome and Introductions, Program Overview, Your Learning Zone, Leadership Characteristics, Managing and Leading, 360 degree feedback, Values and Coaching, and What's Next.

### The Learning Cycle

A model of how we move through competence and choice. It maps four states and the choices that move you between them:

```
        I'm OK at this / Not interested / It's not that important
                          ↑   ↑
                          |   |  (choice to disengage)
   ┌──────────────────────┴───┴──────────────────────┐
   │  I KNOW WHAT I        →(choice)→     LEARNING     │
   │  DON'T KNOW                                       │
   │       ↑                                    ↓      │
   │  I DON'T KNOW         ←(choice)←     HABITS /     │
   │  WHAT I DON'T KNOW                   EXPERTISE    │
   └──────────────────────────────────────────────────┘
```

The cycle shows that growth begins when you become aware of a gap ("I know what I don't know"), make the choice to move into Learning, and then convert that learning into Habits and Expertise. At each point there is a competing pull to opt out ("I'm OK at this", "not interested", "it's not that important").

### The Learning Zone

To grow, you need to step beyond what is familiar and comfortable.

| Comfort Zone | Learning Zone |
| --- | --- |
| Known, easy, familiar, "can do" | New, "don't know how (yet)", stretch, challenge, uncomfortable, unfamiliar |

> [!key] Key idea
> If you stay only in your comfort zone, its boundaries shrink and you become comfortable with less and less. If you step into the learning zone, the boundaries widen and your comfort zone expands to include new territory. (Illustrated by Kirk Fisher's example of teaching his son to drive: what is effortful and full-alert today becomes effortless in time.)

The learning zone is meant to feel like working with a personal trainer who actually asks you to lift weights and build a sweat. Real growth requires participation, asking questions, and engaging rather than staying safe.

> [!note] Cultural lens
> What counts as "learning" and appropriate participation differs across cultures. In some contexts, disagreeing with a superior in a learning setting is seen as disrespectful, so a person may choose silence rather than create friction. Silence does not mean a person has nothing to contribute. When someone is not stepping out of their comfort zone, consider whether cultural beliefs and a preference for reflective listening over active, hands-on learning may be at play.

### Managing the Heat

A parallel leadership skill drawn from Ronald Heifetz's adaptive leadership. Using the metaphor of cooking, the "heat" of a situation rises in a crisis. The leader's task is to keep people in a productive middle zone rather than letting things get either too hot or too cool.

- **Too hot (crisis):** people are on hyper-alert and can only sustain this briefly. The leader needs to bring the temperature down to something manageable.
- **Too cool (comfort zone):** people reach for a technical fix to what is really a complex (adaptive) problem, laugh it off, or avoid the hard issue.
- **The productive middle zone:** people are engaged with the problem, there is a sense of urgency (a term borrowed from John Kotter), and the leader invites the difficult issues and "the outside in" (including views people may not want to hear).

> [!tip] Sustainability
> We cannot stay in the learning zone indefinitely. Thinking of the prefrontal cortex, neuroscience (Dr David Rock and others) suggests roughly four hours a day is the limit for sustained learning-zone work. When it is time to do the real work, the middle "cooking" zone is where the good work happens.

### The Balcony and the Dance Floor

Another adaptive leadership idea from Heifetz. On the **dance floor** you are caught up in the noise, motion, politics and operational rush of day-to-day work (often addictive). Going up to the **balcony** lets you look down and see the patterns: how the work is really going, whether people are clustering, whether you are staying true to your stated values and core purpose, or getting seduced into activity disconnected from your purpose.

**The GLOBE (2004) study** details universally desirable and undesirable leader traits.

- *Desirable traits:* trustworthy, intelligent, honest, plans ahead, encouraging, positive, dynamic, motivator, confidence builder, dependable, just, decisive, effective bargainer, win-win problem solver, skilled administrator, communicator, informed, team builder.
- *Undesirable traits:* loner, antisocial, ruthless, not cooperative, egocentric, dictatorial, non-explicit.

GLOBE identified nine leadership styles and the styles preferred by particular cultures. For example, participative leadership tends to be preferred by Anglo cultures and disliked by Confucian Asian clusters. Ideal leadership therefore looks different in different countries: decision making, communication style and dark-side tendencies are influenced by the region in which a leader operates.

> [!important] Leading across cultures
> When leading in a country that is not your own, it is important to adapt to the team you are leading. When selecting someone to lead overseas, competencies beyond technical ability matter, especially openness and empathy.

> [!quote] On finding your own way
> There is no tick-box checklist that guarantees great leadership, because everyone does it differently and you are trying to find your authentic self. A useful starting point is to ask: who is the best leader I have ever worked for, and what did I see that person doing? (Janet Horton, drawing on a manager who connected her waitressing experience to handling mistakes: "that's good, because we spill a lot of beer around here.")

### Management versus Leadership

We often get caught up in the busyness of day-to-day management and leave little space for leadership. Management activities are relatively immediate and comfortable and give quick wins. Leadership takes time and investment in relationships, vision and strategic thinking. Both are needed, but leadership is usually the one that suffers, especially in times of crisis or when facing unknown challenges.

*Adapted from Heifetz and Laurie (1997):*

| Activity | Managing | Leading |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Direction** | Provide direction | Frame key questions |
| **Protection** | Shield from threats | Let pressure be felt |
| **Control Conflict** | Restore order | Expose and let emerge |
| **Shape Norms** | Maintain norms | Challenge norms |
| **Orientation** | Clarify responsibility | Challenge and disorient |

> [!note] Reflection prompts
> Am I more of a manager or a leader, and how do I feel about that? When do you need to manage and when do you need to lead, and why?

### Core Leadership Competencies

The WLA Management and Leadership framework groups competencies under four domains:

| Domain | Competencies |
| --- | --- |
| **Driving Future Direction** | Providing strategic direction; Driving change; Facilitating innovation and improvement; Monitoring the business environment |
| **Achieving Performance Goals** | Driving performance; Managing tasks and processes; Using judgement and analysis |
| **Building Organisational Culture** | Communicating effectively; Facilitating teamwork; Developing others; Driving organisational values |
| **Enhancing Personal Effectiveness** (central) | Acting with integrity; Knowing and developing self; Managing proactively; Showing adaptability and resilience |

Enhancing Personal Effectiveness sits at the centre, connecting and underpinning the other three domains.

### Personal Leadership Vision

An invitation to define the leader you want to become: What is your vision for yourself as a leader? What are your aspirations? What do you want to achieve? How do you want to be known? You are encouraged to create an image of yourself as a leader in whatever way works best for you, whether a drawing, symbols, words or a story.

### Values and Values in Action

> [!quote] Ulrich et al. (1999)
> "Values inform desired results when leaders ask the question: what is the right thing to do in this case? When they find a clear answer to this question, leaders produce lasting results."

**Universal values do exist.** Seligman's work developing the VIA Strengths survey identified six broad virtue categories that are universal:

- Wisdom
- Courage
- Humanity
- Justice
- Temperance
- Transcendence

Differences in how these six categories are expressed exist across cultures, and they are measured and tracked by organisations such as Transparency International and Edelman.

**Values in Action (the T-shirt metaphor).** The value you hold strongly (on the front of your T-shirt: "what I say") can differ from what people actually see you doing (on the back: "what I do"). Examples from the workshop:

- Front: *"My family is the most important thing in the world."* Back: *"These late-night meetings and work on the weekends are really getting me noticed."*
- Front: *"I value creativity, innovation and risk-taking."* Back: *"Don't you dare stuff up."*
- Front: a value of honesty. Back: avoiding open and honest feedback when the opportunity arises.

> [!key] Why this matters
> People evaluate your leadership by what they can see, not by what your values are. If what you do does not match what you say, it is hard for people to trust you. The exercise: pick one of your values, identify when it comes into conflict, name what people see on the back of your T-shirt, and work out what you can do to close that gap.

### The PERMAH Model

Professor Martin Seligman developed the **PERMA** model of wellbeing (Positive Emotion, Engagement, Relationships, Meaning, Accomplishment). Researchers including Michelle McQuaid and Peggy Kern found that **Health** was missing (eating well, moving regularly, sleeping deeply all significantly affect wellbeing), so Health was added to make the **PERMAH** model. Different countries and cultures place different levels of emphasis on the dimensions.

| Letter | Dimension | In brief |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **P** | Positive Emotions | Joy, hope, happiness; reframing the way we look at the world |
| **E** | Engagement | Leverage the things you are good at; being in a state of "flow" where time stops |
| **R** | Relationships | Belonging, being part of a community, social capital, cultivate relationships |
| **M** | Meaning | Recognise the impact of what you do on other people and things |
| **A** | Accomplishment | Learn from what we do, develop grit and a growth mindset, celebrate achievements |
| **H** | Health (new) | Physical and mental health |

> [!note] Context
> Relationships is arguably the most important component. A key challenge is that individualistic societies erode community belonging and social capital, which can be detrimental to wellbeing and resilience. The PERMAH model has been validated with more than 16,000 participants and is used in organisations to diagnose wellbeing and develop a growth mindset.

### Resilience and Building Resilience

> [!quote] Sutcliffe and Vogus (2003)
> "Resilience is the capacity to rebound from adversity strengthened and more resourceful."

- Resilience is a skill that can be learned and developed.
- It begins with knowing yourself: your strengths and weaknesses, and your triggers (what are you like when you are under the pump?).
- In high-pressure, challenging times you need to be personally resilient and look after yourself proactively and "in the moment".

## W1.2 Executive Ready Program 360° Feedback Tool

To gain greater awareness of your leadership impact, before the first workshop you administer a 360° Feedback Tool. The questionnaire is based on the WLA framework for Management and Leadership and its four domains:

- Building organisational culture
- Achieving performance goals
- Driving future direction
- Enhancing personal effectiveness

### How to Administer

You complete the questionnaire yourself and ask three others (a subordinate, colleague or boss) to complete it for you. Choose people with whom your working relationships are important and whose feedback you value and could truly learn from.

You will need to make copies of:

- **Respondent instructions** (three copies, one per respondent)
- **Participant instructions** (one copy for yourself)
- **Feedback worksheet** (four copies: one for yourself and one for each respondent)

Ask respondents to complete the feedback and return it in an envelope. If you email respondents, bring a printed copy in an envelope to the program. It is best to wait until the program before attempting to interpret the results. Filling in the questionnaire should take no more than 10 to 15 minutes. Be honest with yourself.

> [!important] Not anonymous
> The respondent feedback is not anonymous. Respondents are asked to include their name where indicated and to be as honest as possible.

### Rating Categories

You can rate yourself (and respondents rate you) in one of the following categories:

| Rating | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| **Real strength** | Does it easily; proven skill over numerous occasions to many audiences |
| **Approaches effectiveness** | Does it well most of the time; could be strengthened occasionally but not beyond proven capabilities |
| **Development area** | An area where development and improvement is needed |
| **NA** | An area that is not applicable for your role |

### Why Follow-up Matters: Perception of Change

The value of 360° feedback rises sharply with what you do after you receive it. Adapted from Seligman (2012) and McQuaid and Kern (2018), perceived change in effectiveness (scored out of 5):

| Approach | Perceived change |
| --- | --- |
| Feedback solicited, no sharing, no follow-up | **1.2** |
| Feedback shared and plan for change, but no follow-up | **2.3** |
| Feedback shared, plan for change, and regular follow-up | **3.5** |

### Reviewing Your Feedback

For each respondent, take out your self-assessment, compare their assessment with yours, and record your reactions:

- **Agree** : items you and your respondent agree are real strengths.
- **Questions** : items that provoke further questions or that you want to learn more about (for example an unexpected rating).
- **Improvement needed** : items either of you placed in the development area, and items in "approaches effectiveness" that you want to move up to "real strength".

### Tips for Getting Feedback

- Ask questions in such a way that the most hesitant person would feel comfortable answering them.
- Take responsibility for your own behaviour.
- Acknowledge that you sometimes see yourself the same way.
- Ask for their assistance and suggestions.
- Be genuinely interested in hearing a contrary point of view.

## W1.6 Peer Coaching Session 1: Submission

### Establishing a Coaching Relationship

Based on Suzi Finkelstein's guidance, establishing a coaching relationship is dynamic, ongoing and worth investing in. The relationship is set up with care because it takes courage and vulnerability to share in this way, and people arrive with questions, expectations and even anxieties. The key elements:

1. **Build rapport.** Find out a little about each other, answer questions, and offer validation. Feeling connected lets people relax out of defensiveness into a more constructive space. Rapport must be authentic, relevant and appropriate. Notice the people you connect with and how they validate you, and borrow those strategies.
2. **Build trust.** This is the second most important element. When you feel comfortable with someone you are naturally more inclined to explore and be inquisitive. Consider what it takes for you to trust someone.
3. **Understand their context.** Capture the details that matter. Ask clarifying questions to gain clarity on the world your coachee operates in. You do not need every detail, just enough to stand beside them and explore together. Consider the "rhythm of their world".
4. **Listen and reflect back.** Holding up the mirror and reflecting what you hear can create awareness that was not present before, and can reveal potential conflict or tension.
5. **Ask permission to challenge.** Before respectfully challenging any inconsistencies you observe, ask permission and be clear about it. Gaining this permission is an important part of establishing the relationship.
6. **Clarify the coaching contract (their "why").** Understand why they embarked on the course and what they hope to gain from the program and the coaching. Each session also has its own contract: what does your coachee want to gain from this session? Clarifying this keeps you, as coach, on course.

## W1.7 Post-workshop: Additional readings and resources

### Reading 1: Leading Across Cultures Requires Flexibility and Curiosity (Deborah Rowland, HBR, 2016)

Rowland's research (88 in-depth behavioural event interviews coding change leadership with 65 leaders across multiple industries and five continents) found that the number one skill for leading across complex global organisations is the ability to **tune into a culture without pre-conceived biases or judgement** and work with different world views.

Key ideas:

- **See the world as others see it.** Fostering cross-cultural understanding takes dedicated effort. Even watching local television, especially comedy, can reveal how a different culture works. One CEO of an international charity worked across 50 cultures by meeting in each other's homes, travelling together, and doing job swaps ("a period of walking in each other's shoes").
- **Do not completely assimilate.** Successful global leaders keep a healthy level of detachment so they can see a culture's patterns clearly. Embracing a culture never means ignoring its ingrained habits. One executive used cultural difference to challenge assumptions by tapping into local pride while remaining non-judgemental, leading a successful turnaround without local leaders feeling culturally imposed upon.
- **Freedom within a framework.** Use a metaphor that holds a global frame while allowing local adaptation. A leader at Shell united a commercial fuels organisation across almost 100 countries as one "armada" (a fleet united in endeavour, each fighting its own local battles), communicated through pictorial Learning Maps. Each region adapted the metaphor to its own context (the Asian region described itself as a fierce fleet of dragon boats). The metaphor created a shared language with different local translations.

### Reading 2: What Leadership Looks Like in Different Cultures (Tomas Chamorro-Premuzic and Michael Sanger, HBR, 2016)

The core ingredients of leadership are universal (good judgement, integrity, people skills), but the full recipe requires "culture-specific condiments", because cultures differ in their implicit theories of leadership. Good leadership is largely **personality in the right place**. The article reviews six leadership types across three areas:

**Decision making**

| Type | Where preferred | Tendencies |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Synchronised leader** | Northeast Asia (China, South Korea, Japan), Indonesia, Thailand, UAE, much of Latin America | Seeks consensus, strong process orientation; cycles can be longer but deals must close fast once everyone is onboard; prudent, more focused on threats than rewards |
| **Opportunistic leader** | Germanic and Nordic Europe, the UK and UK-influenced Western countries (US, Australia, New Zealand), and British-model Asian countries (India, Singapore, Malaysia, Hong Kong) | Self-initiating, flexible, thrives in ambiguity; should check in frequently so others keep up; ambitious risk-takers |

**Communication style**

| Type | Where preferred | Tendencies |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Straight-shooting leader** | Northeast Asia, the Netherlands | Confronts issues directly, task-oriented, addresses problems as soon as observed; less interpersonally sensitive |
| **Diplomatic leader** | New Zealand, Sweden, Canada, much of Latin America | Keeps conversations pleasant, handles confrontation with empathy, continuously gauges audience reactions; polite and agreeable |

**Dark-side tendencies**

| Type | Where more tolerated | Tendencies |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **"Kiss up / kick down" leader** | Western Asia (Turkey, India, UAE), Serbia, Greece, Kenya, South Korea | Excessive deference upward, fiery or uncompromising downward; diligent with bosses, dominating with reports |
| **Passive-aggressive leader** | Indonesia, Malaysia | Cynical, mistrusting, covertly resistant under stress; critical and resentful; aversion to conflict ironically generates conflict |

> [!note] Adapting your style
> Any individual can adjust their leadership style to fit context, but it takes real effort to go against natural tendencies, and habits are hard to break. Organisational culture also matters and needs granular analysis. When senior leaders succeed, they often redefine culture in their own image, so culture becomes the sum of the values and beliefs of influential past leaders.

### Resource: Women in Leadership

The workshop materials include perspectives on women's leadership.

- **Julia Gillard and Ngozi Okonjo-Iweala** share standout lessons: there is no right way to be a woman leader, so be true to yourself; war-game the gendered moments in advance with mentors and supporters (forewarned is forearmed); call out and debunk gender stereotypes; support systems and changes that aid gender equality even before you personally need them; do not take a backwards step or assume you are too junior; and ultimately, "go for it". They also discuss the **glass cliff** phenomenon, where women are more likely to be appointed to lead organisations already in crisis.
- **On power** (from leading women): power is knowing who you are and using your voice; it is not given to you but is something you unlock; it is about taking people on a journey to an outcome, knowing your intent and your "why", living life on your own terms, and making an impact on others' lives.
- **Breaking the glass ceiling** (Growth Faculty): as recently as 1995 there were no female CEOs on the Fortune 500 list; by 2023 women made up 8.8% of those CEOs and only around 23% of executives worldwide. The piece profiles eight inspirational leaders (Emmeline Pankhurst, Indra Nooyi, Angela Merkel, Sheryl Sandberg, Melanie Perkins, Michelle Obama, Ruth Bader Ginsburg, Sara Blakely) and notes research suggesting women often outrank men in most leadership skills, especially soft skills such as relationship building, empathy and initiative, and may lead particularly well in a crisis.

## W1.8 Workshop 1 Conclusion

Workshop 1 establishes the foundation for the whole program by asking *what sort of leader do I want to be?* The threads tie together:

- **Authenticity is the anchor.** There is no single checklist for great leadership; the work is to find your authentic self, model it on the best leaders you have known, and close the gap between your espoused values and your visible behaviour.
- **Self-awareness drives growth.** The Learning Cycle and Learning Zone show that growth requires stepping out of comfort, and the 360° feedback process (with genuine follow-up) is the practical engine for that self-awareness.
- **Leading is different from managing.** Both matter, but leadership (framing questions, challenging norms, letting pressure be felt) takes deliberate investment that the busyness of management can crowd out.
- **Wellbeing and resilience underpin it all.** The PERMAH model and resilience practices keep you resourceful enough to lead through pressure.
- **Development is relational.** Peer coaching and developing others are core leadership talents you practise across the program.

You leave with personal commitments (WWW, EBI, a possible 30 day challenge) and a leadership vision to carry into the next workshop and "apply on the job".

## Reflection Questions

1. Who is the best leader you have ever worked for, and what specific behaviours made them so? Are you demonstrating leadership traits that are universally understood and respected?
2. Am I more of a manager or a leader, and how do I feel about that? When do I need to manage and when do I need to lead?
3. Pick one of your top five values. When does it come into conflict, what would people see on the back of your T-shirt, and what can you do to close that gap?
4. Where do you sit in each PERMAH dimension, and where could you focus more energy?
5. What are your triggers when you are "under the pump", and how will you look after your resilience proactively?
6. How will you "manage the heat" of this leadership journey as a learner?
7. To what extent do you need to adapt your leadership to the needs of a multicultural team, and how far should one adapt?
8. What is your vision for yourself as a leader, and how do you want to be known?
